Service Management · Chapter 12

How long a wait feels often matters more than how long it is.

Queues form whenever demand briefly exceeds capacity, and in services, where arrivals and service times vary, that happens even when capacity is ample on average. This module covers the cost of waiting, why queues form, Maister's psychology of waiting, the five features of every queuing system, and the exponential–Poisson description of random arrivals.

The $100 bill for $80

A Chicago bank "sold" $100 bills for $80 to the first 35 people in line. The four brothers at the front waited 17 hours for $20 each, about $1.29 an hour; the 35th person waited 9 hours and earned $2.22 an hour. Waiting time is the price people pay for a "free" good.

The longest queue in Indonesia

The waiting list for regular Hajj departure runs to well over 20 years in many provinces. It is still a queue: a calling population, an arrival process, a fixed annual quota (capacity) and a first-registered, first-served discipline.

Unit 01

The economics of waiting

LO1

Waiting has two price tags. For a firm, an internal customer who waits (an employee at the copier) costs unproductive wages. For an external customer, the cost is the forgone alternative use of that time, plus boredom and anxiety. Its true cost is hard to measure, so the trade-off between waiting cost and service cost is seldom made explicit.

Lost sales

Customers balk

Drivers pass a filling station with a long line. Firms conceal the queue: restaurants divert diners to the bar, Disneyland sells tickets outside the park, casinos snake the line for shows through the slot machines.

Customer as resource

Use the wait

A waiting patient completes a medical history and saves physician time; diners make their own salad while the cook prepares the meal. The wait adds capacity.

Inventory of customers

Utilization is bought with waiting

A queue is like work-in-process inventory: it keeps servers busy. Post offices, clinics and welfare offices achieve high utilization with long queues.

When waiting is life or death

During COVID-19, some patients died waiting for a ventilator or ICU bed. On Mount Everest in 2019, the queue in the "death zone" exhausted climbers' oxygen. Such consequences should change capacity planning and inventory policy.

Example 12.1 · Accommodating elite flyers

Airlines give elite flyers a special phone number to a deliberately over-staffed service center. The extra cost buys zero waiting, because these high-revenue customers can easily take their business to a competitor.

The cost of waiting in Indonesia

At busy referral hospitals, BPJS Kesehatan patients have often arrived before dawn to secure a queue number: the cost is a lost working day for the patient and often a family member too. A lorry stuck for hours at a port gate or weighbridge carries a direct cost (driver wages, demurrage) that a firm can measure. Which of these waits is easier to justify reducing in a budget meeting, and why?

Unit 02

How queues form

LO2

A queue is a line of customers waiting for one or more servers. It forms whenever current demand exceeds the existing capacity to serve, which is bound to happen when arrivals occur at varying times and service times also vary, even if average capacity is higher than average demand.

Variation 1

Bulk service

Servers need not serve one customer at a time: buses, airplanes and elevators serve in batches.

Variation 2

Server travels

The server may come to the customer: fire, police and ambulance services.

Variation 3

Queues in series

Stages of queues or a network: the haunted-house ride with a walk, a vestibule and then the ride itself.

A queue need not be a physical line: a caller on hold is in a virtual queue.

Unit 03

The psychology of waiting

LO3LO4

David Maister was one of the first to study the human side of waiting. Burger King's early "snakes" (get in line and wait, and wait) show why it matters: once competition grew, managing the wait became a competitive edge.

Maister's first law of service

Satisfaction = Perception − Expectation

Better-than-expected service sends a happy customer away to tell friends (a "Facebook effect"). The trickle-down works both ways: a bad experience makes a better story to pass along.

Maister's second law of service

It is hard to play catch-up ball.

First impressions color the whole experience. If customers must wait, make that first wait pleasant, because it is hard to recover later.

The four "attributes of waiting" in LO4 are the four headed rows (2–5): empty time, pre-process waiting, uncertainty and unfairness.

Burger King's three line-ups

Conventional: one line, one register; assemblers at the far end. Slow.
Hospitality: several registers, customers pick a line; cashier also assembles. Flexible but labor-intensive, and "wrong line" disappointment.
Multiconventional: back to one line, but a register that records up to six orders at once; assemblers prepare orders. Fair (FCFS) and fast.

Waiting psychology in Indonesia

Light at the end of the tunnel: KRL platform displays count down to the next train, and the Mobile JKN app shows a patient's queue number and estimated call time. Fairness: priority counters and seats for older people, pregnant women and people with disabilities are widely accepted because the rule is visible and explained. Empty time: a bengkel (motorcycle workshop) that offers a TV, Wi-Fi and a glass of tea, or lets you watch the mechanic work, is using Maister's ideas without the label.

Essential features of a queuing system

LO5

Figure 12.1 describes every queuing system with five features: (1) calling population, (2) arrival process, (3) queue configuration, (4) queue discipline and (5) service process, followed by departure.

FeatureThe question it answersClassification options (Figures 12.2, 12.8, 12.10, 12.11, 12.13)
Calling populationWho can arrive?Homogeneous or several subpopulations (walk-ins, appointments, emergencies); finite (three secretaries sharing a copier) or infinite
Arrival processWhen do they arrive?Controllable (appointments, prices) or uncontrollable; random (often exponential between arrivals) or scheduled; arrivals may balk
Queue configurationHow do they wait?Multiple queues, single queue, take-a-number, virtual queue; finite or unlimited waiting room; customers may renege or jockey
Queue disciplineWho is served next?Static (FCFS) or dynamic (SPT, cμ, priority classes, preemptive priority, round-robin)

Example 12.2 · Queuing at Starbucks

You arrive on foot at a random time (arrival process) from a potential customer group (calling population). Seeing the line, you may leave (balk) or join one of several possible configurations (queue configuration). In line you might give up and leave (renege). You are selected by FCFS (queue discipline), your drink is made and paid for by card (service process), and you leave (departure), perhaps to return.

Map a Puskesmas visit

Calling population: residents of the catchment area, split into general patients, mothers and children, and emergencies. Arrival process: a morning surge, partly controlled by online booking. Configuration: one registration queue, then separate queues at each poli. Discipline: FCFS within each poli, triage for emergencies. Service process: registration → examination → pharmacy, servers in series. Try mapping your faculty's academic office the same way.

Unit 05

The arrival process: exponential and Poisson

LO6

Record arrival times, compute the times between arrivals, and you will often find an exponential distribution: many short gaps, a long tail, and a standard deviation about equal to the mean (μ = 2.4 and σ = 2.6 minutes in the university clinic of Figure 12.3). Count arrivals per hour instead and the same process is Poisson.

Exponential (time between arrivals, continuous)

f(t) = λe−λt   F(t) = 1 − e−λt, t ≥ 0

Mean = 1/λ, variance = 1/λ². F(t) is the probability that the next arrival comes within t.

Clinic: mean gap 2.4 min → λ = 1/2.4 = 0.4167 per minute (25 per hour). F(5) = 1 − e−0.4167×5 = 1 − 0.124 = 0.876.

Poisson (number of arrivals, discrete)

f(n) = (λt)n e−λt / n!, n = 0, 1, 2, …

Mean = variance = λt. Gives the probability of n arrivals in an interval t.

Clinic: λ = 25 per hour, t = 1: the chance of no patients in an hour is e−25 = 1.4 × 10−11.

The equivalence (Figure 12.4)

Exponential times between arrivals with mean 2.4 minutes is the same process as Poisson arrivals with mean 60/2.4 = 25 per hour. Just keep λ and t in the same time unit. Also check that λ is stationary: ambulance calls vary by hour of day (Figure 12.5), clinic arrivals by day of week (Figure 12.6), air travel by month (Figure 12.7), and demand can also shift across space (ambulance calls move from homes to offices during working hours).

Is your λ stationary?

A campus food court near ITS is quiet at 10:00, packed at 12:00, and dead again during Friday prayers. Averaging the whole day into one λ hides the peak that actually decides how many cashiers are needed. Measure λ per hour (or per half hour), and per day of the week, before using any queuing formula.

Unit 06

Queue configuration, queue discipline and the service process

LO4LO5

Queue configuration (Figure 12.9)

(a) Multiple queues

Customers pick a line and may jockey. Advantages: differentiated service (express lanes), division of labor (experienced teller on the commercial lane), choice of a preferred server, and less balking than at one long snake.

(b) Single queue

Velvet ropes, one sinuous line. Advantages: guaranteed FCFS fairness, no "wrong line" anxiety, no cutting in and little reneging, more privacy at the counter, and a lower average wait.

(c) Take a number

No formal line: customers wander, chat, sit, or browse the pastries (impulse sales). But they must listen for their number.

Virtual queue

On hold, with no visible position: the most frustrating. Some call centers announce the caller's position.

Finite queue

No room left, so arrivals are turned away: a parking garage showing FULL.

Online check-in

Great Clips customers check in by app; walk-ins thought others cut ahead, until a monitor showed everyone's order of "arrival."

Queue discipline (Figure 12.11)

FCFS (first-come, first-served) is static: only position in line matters. It is egalitarian.

Dynamic disciplines use an attribute of the customer or the queue:

  • SPT (shortest processing time): minimizes the average time in the system, but long jobs keep getting pushed back.
  • cμ rule: serve in decreasing order of c × μ (delay cost × service rate, i.e., c ÷ service time). It maximizes the joint benefit to customer and provider; ideal inside one organization, where c is known.
  • Priority classes with FCFS inside each: express check-out; triage.
  • Preemptive priority: interrupt a service for a higher priority (an ambulance on a routine transfer diverted to a cardiac arrest).
  • Round-robin: a dentist alternates between patients while anesthetic takes effect.

With multiple queues and jockeying, FCFS cannot be guaranteed.

Screening at airports

TSA PreCheck (Trusted Traveler) prescreens volunteers so they use an expedited lane without removing shoes, liquids or laptops. Delta and TSA's 5-bin loading lets five passengers load bins at once, so one slow passenger doesn't hold up the line.

Bus signal priority

Transit agencies give late buses a green-light extension or a "queue jump" phase at intersections, a priority discipline applied to traffic.

Triage in Indonesian hospitals

Emergency departments (IGD) sort patients by color category, so a patient with chest pain is seen before one with a sprained ankle who arrived earlier. Explaining the rule (a poster, a word from the triage nurse) is Maister's fairness strategy at work.

Service process

Service times can follow any distribution (Figure 12.12). A car wash is nearly constant; brief, simple services (fast-food orders, tolls, supermarket check-outs) are often exponential, as are the clinic's short "second-service" visits. The distribution reflects variation in both customer needs and server performance.

Server behavior matters: under pressure, servers speed up and become curt, and quality falls. A busy 911 operator who dispatches a patrol car without screening the call wastes scarce capacity.

Parallel servers let managers open and close lines as demand changes; cross-training (stockers who become cashiers) adds flexibility; and parallel servers give redundancy if equipment fails.

Service benchmark · The magic of Disney makes queues disappear

FastPass (1999) gave guests a one-hour return window for a popular ride; FastPass+ (2012) let resort guests reserve before arriving and use RFID wristbands. Guests spend the saved time in restaurants and shops. Disney also gained a way to fill slow periods and to anticipate ride demand. Indonesian theme parks such as Dufan sell fast-track tickets, a paid version of the same idea. Is charging for it fair to everyone else in the line?

Case 12.1 · Thrifty Car Rental

Assignment: describe the five essential features of the queuing systems at the counter, the garage and the car wash.

Case 12.2 · Eye'll Be Seeing You

Mrs. F arrives early for a 1:30 PM eye appointment, watches later arrivals taken in first, is moved to a dilating area without drops, and is told "Go sit down" each time she asks. At 4:00 PM she leaves and writes to Dr. X that her family is moving its care elsewhere. Questions: how do Maister's laws apply, what was good and bad about the waiting process, how should Dr. X reply, and how can such incidents be prevented?

Case 12.3 · Field study

With a stopwatch, study a real waiting line (post office, fast-food outlet, bank). Sketch the layout and queue configuration, describe the calling population and discipline, and collect enough interarrival and service times to test whether they are exponential. ID twist: a Samsat office, an academic-services counter at ITS, or a busy warung at lunch all work well.

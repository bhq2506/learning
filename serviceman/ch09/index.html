Service Management · Chapter 9

In services, the customer is also a supplier.

Goods move along a chain from supplier to customer. Services work on something the customer brings (their mind, body, belongings or information), so the "chain" becomes a two-way relationship. This module contrasts the two, then covers the sources of value in service relationships, social media, professional service firms and outsourcing.

Start studyingTry the bullwhip simulator

Postponement at HP and Taco Bell

HP made one generic printer and let distributors add the local manual and power cord, pooling demand and cutting inventory. Taco Bell moved cooking out of the stores to central kitchens: stores switched from make-to-order to assemble-to-order, with shorter waits, cleaner stores and more seats.

Same idea, Indonesia

Many food chains run central kitchens that send marinated, portioned ingredients to outlets, so each outlet only cooks and assembles. Coffee chains send pre-mixed bases to kiosks so a barista can finish a drink in under a minute.

Unit 01

Supply chains for physical goods

LO1LO2

The goods supply chain is a network of value-adding stages (suppliers → manufacturing → distribution → retailing → recycling) with material flowing downstream and inventory between stages. Information flows upstream: point-of-sale data can warn the factory before inventory piles up or sales are lost.

Bullwhip effect

In an uncoordinated chain, a small change in retail orders is magnified at each stage upstream. Stages don't see real final demand and overreact; delays create oscillations, so overstocks are followed by stockouts.

Managing uncertainty

Three sources: supplier delivery, manufacturing reliability and customer demand (the hardest). Safety stock is the insurance. Better quality control, more dependable transport and postponement reduce the need for it.

Sustainability

Product-life-cycle management adds a recycling stage: remanufactured printer cartridges, recycled aluminum cans, cars designed for disassembly.

Try it · Bullwhip simulator (simplified)

Four stages each forecast the orders they receive and order enough to restore a target stock after a lead time. Customer demand is about 100 a week and rises 20% from week 6. Watch how orders swing more at each stage upstream, then switch on the textbook's remedies.

From conflict to collaboration: P&G and Walmart (Example 9.1)

P&G ran price promotions on Pampers; Walmart forward-bought at the discount and stopped ordering for months. The result: a self-inflicted bullwhip and swings in P&G's production. The fix: P&G stopped promotions and Walmart adopted everyday low prices, with shared data and continuous replenishment (Table 9.1).

Omnichannel supply chains and supply-chain design

Omnichannel

One central stock pool serves every channel (store, web, app, marketplace) with inventory visible regardless of channel. Pooling lowers inventory, raises service levels and lets customers track orders. "The Amazon effect" pushed every retailer this way: browse, buy and return through any channel.

ID · Brands sell through their own stores, marketplaces and live-stream shopping from one warehouse; minimarkets act as pickup and payment points for online orders.

COVID-19 and Indonesian supply chains

In early 2020, masks and hand sanitizer vanished from shelves: an "efficient" supply chain hit by a demand shock and then a supply shock, exactly the shift toward agile design in Figure 9.3. The cooking-oil shortages of 2022 showed a supply-side shock to a staple that normally runs on an efficient chain. Which of today's "efficient" chains would you redesign?

Unit 02

Customer–supplier duality: hubs, not chains

LO1

Every service acts on something the customer supplies: their mind (education, entertainment), body (transport, lodging, health care), belongings (repair, cleaning, banking) or information (tax, insurance, legal). So the customer is also a supplier, and relationships run in both directions.

Hubs, not chains

Relationships beyond two levels are rare. The provider acts as the customer's agent with outside suppliers, so there are fewer delays and information is shared easily. Partnering or sole-sourcing is common (physician and lab).

Capacity is the inventory

Customer inputs arrive randomly and expect immediate processing, and services can't be stockpiled, so reserve capacity (or reservations) plays the role inventory plays for goods.

Inputs vary in quality

Incomplete tax documents, "I feel sick", undisclosed house defects. Explicitly communicate what the customer must supply before the service starts.

ID · Two-level relationships

A patient sees a doctor at a puskesmas, who sends blood to a referral laboratory. A homebuyer applies for a mortgage (KPR) and the bank checks the land certificate with the land office (BPN) through a notary. A motorbike goes to a bengkel, which sends the cylinder head to a specialist machinist.

ID · Poor customer inputs

Student scholarship applications missing a signed letter; patients who forget their JKN card; online sellers who give couriers incomplete addresses. Checklists sent in advance by WhatsApp are a cheap fix.

Unit 03

Sources of value: bidirectional optimization, productive capacity, perishability

LO3LO4

The chapter uses home health care, where nurses, dietitians and therapists are mobile servers who travel to the patient. Managing the relationship well (Table 9.4) creates value from three sources.

1 · Bidirectional optimization

Do what is best for the customer and the firm at the same time. Instead of "we'll come between 12 and 5", an integrated system offers each patient a few precise slots pre-optimized for the whole team, considering language, skills, licences, supplies and where the worker will be before and after.

2 · Productive capacity

Value is created only at the customer's site; travel time is lost capacity. Three strategies: transfer (an FAQ website), replacement (a home blood-pressure monitor instead of a nurse visit) and embellishment (teach the family to change a dressing; checking every 3 days instead of daily cuts nursing time 66%).

3 · Perishability

Idle worker time is lost forever. Dynamic, real-time schedules assign the next job only when the current one ends. Predicted idle slots are filled with mobile training and certification: a "service inventory hedge".

Unit 04

Social media in services

LO4

Web 1.0 automated routine processes; Web 2.0 builds relationships and co-creates value. Social media is now the first place customers go to contact a service firm. "Today's firms listen first and sell next."

Useful characteristics

Wide reach (decentralized, many producers and consumers), easy access at little or no cost, ease of use, immediacy and flexibility (content can be edited instantly).

Netiquette for financial services

Avoid the three D's: disclosure, defamation, discrimination. Promote the three R's: reciprocation, respect, reliability.

Five strategies to move offline operations online (Onmark)

Tune-up reminders

Tax filing dates, dental check-ups posted where customers already are.

Cookie-cutter presence

Be on every channel customers use for questions, and answer there.

Lead generation

Relationships outside your service lead to referrals; word of mouth spreads fastest online.

Customer education

Promote a lifestyle that aligns with the service: an insurer promotes safe driving.

Start online, finish offline

Move sensitive questions to a phone call or an office visit.

Example 9.2

A snowbound East Village bakery offered half-price hot drinks on Facebook and runs photo contests: when a customer posts, all their friends see the brand.

Convenience and its risks

Mobile check deposits, phone boarding passes; insurers mining Facebook and Twitter to judge risk. Google Maps' unsolicited suggestions and trip reports raise privacy and data-ownership questions. Flip.to found each positive post at booking brought about eight new bookings: consumer-generated media.

Organizing and co-creation

Red Robin launched its Tavern Double burger through an internal social network, producing kitchen-tested recipes in days. Dell's IdeaStorm collected customer ideas such as backlit keyboards.

ID · Chat commerce

Countless Indonesian small businesses take orders, confirm payment and send receipts entirely through WhatsApp Business; catalogs live on Instagram.

ID · Social customer care

Banks, airlines and telecom operators answer complaints on X (Twitter) and Instagram within minutes, then move account details to direct messages: "start online, finish offline".

ID · Live selling

Sellers demonstrate products in live streams and take orders in the comments: co-creation and consumer-generated media in real time.

Unit 05

Professional service firms

LO5

Architects, lawyers, consultants, accountants: knowledge workers who command a body of knowledge, are often licensed, and work in partnerships where partners own the firm and junior professionals aspire to partnership.

Specialized & customized

Work without proven routines.

Face-to-face

Client-management skill matters as much as technical skill.

People are the assets

Attention to recruiting (input) and clients (output).

Four levels of knowledge

Know-what (cognitive) → know-how (advanced skills) → know-why (systems understanding) → care-why (self-motivated creativity).

Service consulting uses the five P's: people (workforce, staffing), processes (reengineering, automation), programs (quality, Lean/Kaizen), plant (facility, layout) and planning and control systems (dispatching, ERP).

A consulting firm is a pyramid of finders (partners who win work), minders (managers who run projects), binders (relationship builders) and grinders (junior consultants doing the legwork).

McKinsey tips (Rasiel)

Underpromise and overdeliver · 80/20 rule · don't boil the ocean · the elevator rule (explain it in 30 seconds) · pluck the low-hanging fruit · hit singles · make a chart every day · engage your client.

Profit per partner = margin × productivity × leverage

Profit/Partner = (Profit/Fees) × (Fees/Staff) × (Staff/Partners)
Productivity = (Fees/Hours) × (Hours/Staff) = value × utilization

Brains projects

Frontier problems; partners are the experts; hard to standardize or leverage. "Hire us because we are smart."

Grey hair projects

Skill and judgment, but repeatable (e.g., ERP implementation); tasks can be delegated. "Hire us because we have done this before."

Procedure projects

Standardized, programmatic (e.g., audits); easily delegated; sold on speed, cost and accuracy. "We know how to do this and can deliver it effectively."

Table 9.5 · Profitability tactics
Margin

Lower fixed (overhead) costs: improve the cash cycle, reduce office space and equipment, reduce admin and support staff.

Productivity

Raise prices and differentiate (specialize, innovate, target higher-value work, invest in training); drop unprofitable services and customers; increase utilization.

Leverage

Lower variable costs: better engagement management, more leverage of professionals, more paraprofessionals.

Unit 06

Outsourcing services

LO6LO7

Outsourcing means an external vendor supplies a service once done in-house. It brings transaction costs: search (finding a capable supplier), bargaining (reaching and writing an agreement) and enforcement (making sure the terms are kept).

Benefits

Focus on core competence (no more kitchen duty for soldiers) · lower cost (competitive janitorial firms) · access to the latest technology without investment (MRI services) · supplier economies of scale (collision repair for car dealers).

Risks

Loss of direct control over quality · employee loyalty and job-loss fears · data security and privacy · dependence on one supplier · coordination costs and delays · atrophy of in-house capability.

Process: need identification → information search → vendor selection → performance evaluation. Services are hard to specify and hard to inspect (how do you know the security contractor is effective?), so selection (experience, reputation, proximity, cost) and evaluation (communication, dependability, flexibility, on-time record) are the main challenges. Arm's-length relationships with codified information suit outsourcing; vertical integration suits activities needing strategic control and durable relationship-specific investment.

Outsourcing (alih daya) in Indonesia

Security guards, cleaning, drivers, catering and call centers are commonly outsourced by banks, hospitals, universities and state-owned enterprises. The rules on what can be outsourced and how outsourced workers are protected changed under the Job Creation Law and its implementing regulations, so legal advice is part of "vendor selection". Notice that these contracts touch every employee personally, which is the textbook's point about services affecting many people at once.

Unit 07

Key terms and cases

Case 9.1 · Boomer Consulting, Inc.

A consultant to accounting firms flew to clients every week for one-on-one projects. To cut travel and standardize, Gary Boomer created Technology Circles: 15–20 similar-sized firms (two people each) meet in Kansas City three times a year. Members lead the breakout sessions; BCI staff present only 2 of 12 hours. 90-day game plans and progress reports create peer accountability; members shape the next agenda. A fine hotel and a members-only website add prestige.

Questions: How do the Circles illustrate bidirectional relationships? How is the client a coproducer? How is leverage achieved? Can the model work in other industries, and with what risks?

Circles in Indonesia

Think of association forums for hospital directors, rectors' forums or MSME (UMKM) mentoring communities. Who could run a paid "circle" for, say, puskesmas heads or small-hotel owners in East Java, and what would keep competitors comfortable sharing?

Case 9.2 · Evolution of B2C e-commerce in Japan

Japan's 50,000+ konbini (7-Eleven alone about 8,000) became the delivery and payment network for online shopping. Customers order online or at an in-store terminal, pay cash at the counter with a barcode slip and pick up a few days later, which overcomes low credit-card use, limited home Internet access and inflexible delivery hours. Mobile phones then let commuters order on the train and collect on the way home.

The Indonesian konbini

Paying for online purchases in cash at a minimarket counter with a payment code, and collecting parcels at a minimarket, became common here for the same reasons: many buyers lacked credit cards, and couriers struggle with addresses.

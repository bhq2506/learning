Service Management · Chapter 6 Supplement

From survey scores to the right fix.

Chapter 6 showed how to measure quality. This module turns measurement into action: find where service falls short, decide which problem to fix first, dig down to its root cause, and write a corrective action plan someone can carry out.

Start with SERVQUALJump to the IPA grid

Why not just fix the lowest score?

A low score on something customers barely care about is not urgent. A modest gap on something they care about a lot can be costing you customers. And fixing a symptom (adding chairs to the waiting room) rarely fixes the cause (no appointment system). You need three answers:

  1. Where are we falling short?SERVQUAL
  2. Which shortfall matters most?IPA, Pareto
  3. Why does it happen, and what will we do?Fishbone, 5 Whys, 5W1H

Unit 01 · Measure

SERVQUAL in depth

LO1LO2

Parasuraman, Zeithaml and Berry built their gap model in 1985 from focus groups across several service industries. In 1988 they published SERVQUAL: a scale that turns GAP 5, the customer's expectation–perception gap, into numbers.

Structure

22 items grouped into 5 dimensions. Each item is asked twice: once about an excellent firm in the industry (expectation, E) and once about this firm (perception, P). Both use a 7-point scale, from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree.

Scoring

Item gap = P − E. A negative gap means a shortfall. A dimension score is the average gap of its items. The unweighted SERVQUAL score is the average of the five dimension scores. The weighted score uses customers' 100-point allocation of importance across the dimensions.

Uses

Track quality over time; compare branches of a multisite firm; benchmark against a competitor; segment customers by their quality perceptions; and, most important for us, find the items with the largest shortfalls.

DimensionItemsWhat it coversTypical weight customers give
Tangibles4 (items 1–4)Facilities, equipment, staff appearance, materialsUsually lowest
Reliability5 (items 5–9)Performing the promised service dependably and accuratelyUsually highest
Responsiveness4 (items 10–13)Willingness to help; prompt serviceHigh
Assurance4 (items 14–17)Knowledge, courtesy, ability to inspire trustMedium–high
Empathy5 (items 18–22)Caring, individualized attentionMedium
Adapting the items

SERVQUAL is a skeleton, not a script. Reword items for the setting ("The clinic has modern-looking medical equipment"), keep one idea per statement, translate carefully (pilot the Bahasa Indonesia version), and add industry-specific items if focus groups reveal them. Keep the five-dimension structure so results stay comparable.

Known criticisms

Difference scores can be statistically unreliable. Expectations asked after the service may be colored by the experience. Asking 44 questions is long. Cronin and Taylor (1992) proposed SERVPERF, which uses perceptions only. SERVQUAL's diagnostic value remains: it shows where expectations are not met.

Unit 02 · Map

Importance-performance analysis (IPA)

LO3

Martilla and James (1977) noticed that studies measured either how important attributes were or how well the firm performed on them, rarely both. Plotting both on one grid gives managers an "easily-understood, low-cost" map of where to invest and where they may be over-investing.

1 · Choose attributes

Missing an attribute customers care about ruins the analysis. Build the list from past research, focus groups, interviews with staff and managers. Keep it manageable. The original auto-dealer study used 14.

2 · Measure both

Ask importance and performance in separate sections of the questionnaire (importance first) so one doesn't bias the other. The original used 4-point scales (extremely to not important; excellent to poor, plus "no basis for judgment").

3 · Plot and split

Performance on the horizontal axis, importance on the vertical. Where to put the crosshairs is "a matter of judgment": the scale midpoint, or the mean of the data when ratings cluster. Medians are theoretically purer, but means gave virtually identical results.

What the 1977 study found

An auto dealer wanted service loyalty to rise from 37% to 50%. 284 of 634 customers (45%) responded. Low prices on service (importance 3.29, performance 2.00) and job done right the first time (3.83, 2.63) landed in "Concentrate here". Sending maintenance notices (2.05, 3.33) looked like possible overkill. With crosshairs at the data means, the grid above reproduces the article's quadrant assignment for all 14 attributes. The authors also advised comparing loyal and disloyal customers: when both groups show the same pattern, confidence in the result rises.

Reading tips (Martilla & James)

Work down the list in order of importance. Look first at the extreme points: they reveal the biggest mismatches. Treat items near the crosshairs with caution, because a small change in ratings or crosshair position moves them to another quadrant.

Later refinement: the diagonal

Later researchers added an iso-priority line (importance = performance). Points above the line have importance exceeding performance, and the farther above, the higher the priority. This helps rank items inside the same quadrant. Toggle it on the grid.

Unit 03 · Rank

Prioritize: combine gaps, importance and complaints

LO4

SERVQUAL tells you how big each shortfall is; IPA tells you how much customers care. Combine them into one ranking, then cross-check against what customers actually complain about.

Priority index

Priority = importance × shortfall, where shortfall = E − P (zero if P ≥ E). An item customers rate 6.6 in importance with a 2.6-point shortfall scores 17.2. An item rated 4.6 with a 0.2 shortfall scores 0.9. Simple, transparent, and easy to explain to managers.

Pareto principle

Often about 80% of complaints come from about 20% of causes. Sort complaint categories from most to least frequent and plot the cumulative percentage. The categories that take the cumulative line to about 80% are the "vital few". Fix those before the "useful many".

Converging evidence

Survey (SERVQUAL), grid (IPA) and complaint log (Pareto) all point to the same problem: long waiting time before seeing the doctor, together with poor information about when service will happen. When three independent sources agree, you can commit resources with confidence. That is the problem we take into root-cause analysis.

Unit 04 · Explore causes

The fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram

LO5

Kaoru Ishikawa's cause-and-effect diagram puts the problem (the effect) at the fish's head and lists possible causes along "bones" grouped into categories. It is a brainstorming and organizing tool. It shows possible causes, not proven ones, so the team must then verify them with data or with the 5 Whys.

Write the head well

State the effect as a specific, measurable problem, not a solution: "Patients wait 95 minutes on average before seeing the doctor (morning session)", not "We need more doctors".

Categories for services

Manufacturing uses the 6Ms (man, machine, method, material, measurement, mother nature). For services, adapt them: People, Process, Policies, Place, Equipment & technology, Information & measurement. Choose whatever categories fit your service.

Run the session

Bring frontline staff: they know the process. Ask "what could cause this?" for each category. No criticism during brainstorming. Then circle the 2–3 most likely causes and test them against data.

Unit 05 · Drill down

The 5 Whys

LO6

Developed at Toyota (Taiichi Ohno), the 5 Whys takes one likely cause from the fishbone and asks "why?" repeatedly until it reaches a cause the organization can fix and that, once fixed, stops the problem from recurring. "Five" is a rule of thumb: sometimes three are enough, sometimes you need six.

Rules

Each answer must be a fact you can check, not a guess. Each "why" asks about the previous answer, not the original problem. Stop when the answer is a process or system cause within your control.

Symptom vs. root cause

"Patients are angry" is a symptom. "The doctor arrives late" is a direct cause. "No one reviews doctor start times against schedules" is a root cause: fix it and the lateness becomes visible and correctable.

Traps

Stopping at a person ("Rina is slow") rather than the system that let it happen. Following only one branch when there are several causes: run a separate chain for each likely cause. Answering from memory rather than going to observe.

"5W" can mean two different tools

The 5 Whys (this unit) finds the root cause. 5W1H (What, Why, Who, Where, When, How) plans the action, and is also used to describe a problem precisely before analysis ("What happens, where, when, to whom, how often?"). Use 5W1H to define the problem, the 5 Whys to find its cause, and 5W1H again to plan the fix.

Unit 06 · Act & verify

Corrective action plan with 5W1H

LO7

A root cause is only useful if someone acts on it. A 5W1H plan makes each action concrete enough to assign, schedule and check. Add a sixth column for the measure, because an action without a success measure can't be verified.

Correction

Fix the immediate problem: apologize and see today's waiting patients first. Necessary, but it doesn't stop recurrence.

Corrective action

Remove the root cause so the problem doesn't recur: align doctors' session times with their real availability.

Preventive action

Stop similar problems elsewhere: review every clinic schedule each quarter, and monitor waiting times on a control chart.

Action plan · Klinik Sehat Sentosa (edit freely; saved in this browser)

Verify (Check in PDCA)

Plan → Do → Check → Act. Track average daily waiting time on an X̄-chart (see Chapter 6, control charts). If the mean drops and stays inside new limits, standardize the change (Act). Re-run SERVQUAL and IPA after 3–6 months: the item should move from "Concentrate here" toward "Keep up the good work".

Make it stick

Update SOPs and train staff on the new process. Tell patients what changed (this also narrows GAP 4). Recognize the team. Then return to the IPA grid and pick the next priority.

Unit 07

Your own improvement project and key terms

Group project brief

  1. Pick a service you can observe, such as a campus canteen, library, health clinic, bank branch, or the KRL or Suroboyo Bus.
  2. Adapt the 22 SERVQUAL items to it, and add an importance rating for each item (separate section, asked first).
  3. Survey at least 30 customers. Compute item and dimension gaps here: paste your means into the tool below.
  4. Map the items on an IPA grid. Justify your crosshair choice.
  5. Prioritize with the priority index and, if available, a complaint Pareto.
  6. Analyze the top problem with a fishbone and one or more 5 Whys chains, verified with observation.
  7. Plan 3–5 corrective actions in 5W1H with measurable targets, and say how you'll verify them.

Use your own data · paste item means (CSV)

One row per item: code,dimension,item text,expectation,perception,importance. Dimension must be T, R, S, A or E (tangibles, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, empathy). Values on your own scale (e.g., 1–7).

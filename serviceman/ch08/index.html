Service Management · Chapter 8

For most services, where is part of what.

Customers usually travel to a service, or the service travels to them, so location shapes demand, cost and competition for years. This module covers location strategy, site selection and GIS, how to model distance and objectives, and three techniques: the cross-median approach, the Huff retail model and location set covering.

Location intelligence at Domino's

A GIS validates every address, settles franchise-territory disputes, adds new housing to delivery areas and routes each phone order to the nearest store. Unknown addresses disappear and delivery gets faster.

Same idea, Indonesia

Food-delivery and ride-hailing apps match every order to a nearby outlet or driver by GPS, and couriers geocode addresses down to the gang. In a country where many addresses are "behind the mosque, second house on the left", location data is a competitive asset.

Unit 01

Strategic location considerations

LO1LO2

A study of why some La Quinta Motor Inns succeeded and others did not found four strategic dimensions of location. Location decisions are long-term, capital-intensive commitments, so they must serve strategy.

Flexibility

Can the location respond to economic, demographic and competitive change? Spread sites across regions (a portfolio) and near inelastic demand, such as a convention center.

Competitive positioning

Multiple sites build awareness; holding prime sites before the market develops is a barrier to entry, like a patent.

Demand management

Control the quantity, quality and timing of demand by locating near diverse market generators that give steady demand all week and all year.

Focus

A standard "formula" facility duplicated at many sites. Risk: sites too close together cannibalize each other, unless growth is planned.

Clustering vs. saturation

Competitive clustering

Locate near your competitors

For shopping goods, customers want to compare, so they go where many sellers are (the "motor mile" of car dealers). La Quinta inns near many competitors had higher occupancy than isolated ones.

Saturation marketing

Locate near yourself

Group many outlets of the same firm tightly in high-traffic areas: Au Bon Pain clustered about 25 cafés in Boston, some under 100 yards apart. Some cannibalization, but less advertising, easier supervision and strong awareness. Works for impulse, non-destination purchases; works better for company-owned outlets than for franchises.

ID · Clustering

Electronics in Glodok, Jakarta; batik and textile wholesalers in Pekalongan and Solo; used-car dealers lining particular streets; and Kampung Inggris in Pare, Kediri, where dozens of English-course providers cluster and students compare them on arrival.

ID · Saturation

National minimarket chains often place outlets within sight of each other, and drink chains open several kiosks in one mall or one street. Each outlet "plants the idea" for a passer-by, like the Helsinki ice-cream carts.

ID · The franchise catch

When outlets belong to different franchisees, the second store across the road takes money from a fellow franchisee, not just from the brand's rivals. Expect conflict unless territories are defined.

Strategies that loosen the link to geography

Marketing intermediaries

Separate entities distribute the service: retailers extend a bank's credit (Visa accepted worldwide), HMOs connect doctors and patients, group insurance through employers.

ID · Branchless-banking agents (a kiosk owner acting as an agent for a bank) bring deposits and transfers to villages without a branch.

Communication for travel

Telemetry extends health care to remote areas; direct payroll deposit saves trips to the bank.

ID · Telemedicine apps; online driving-licence renewal; online tax filing.

Separate front and back office

Dry cleaning, shoe repair, storefront banks. A Texas McDonald's drive-in order may be taken in an Iowa call center (Table 8.1).

ID · Laundry drop-off counters feeding one central plant; banks' centralized back offices.

The Internet

The website is the location. Physical location still matters for shipping (Memphis for FedEx) and for talent; call centers follow the sun.

ID · E-commerce fulfillment centers cluster near toll roads around Jakarta and Surabaya.

E-distance

Online, distance is navigation. An undiscovered site is infinitely distant; five clicks away might lose 90% of visitors, hence the two-click rule. Small New Jersey theaters without a theater district built one shared website: a virtual competitive cluster.

ID · Small homestays in a village gain "clustering" by listing together on one tourism-village website or travel app.

Site considerations and geographic information systems

Geographic information systems: The Sea Ranch

In 2007 a water main broke in The Sea Ranch, a 10-square-mile coastal community in California. Staff drove a 4×4 across 2,000 acres at night, stopping to listen for the leak. Residents missed their morning coffee.

Today a GIS overlays eight data layers: elevation, hydrology, transportation, soils, geology, ownership, site data and imagery. With pressure-sensitive valves, a leak can be found from the office. The same maps support building reviews, commons landscape management, and emergency planning for wildfires, earthquakes and tsunamis, with data backed up locally, off-site and in the cloud.

What GIS does for a service

Planning and analysis; asset and data management; operational awareness across organizational boundaries; support for field units. It turns any spreadsheet data into maps, with live feeds, automated analysis and alerts configured for users from executives to field workers.

ID · One Map Policy

Indonesia's Kebijakan Satu Peta aims to give all ministries one shared reference base map, so that forestry, mining, plantation and village boundaries stop overlapping in conflicting maps.

ID · Disaster risk

BNPB's InaRISK platform maps hazard exposure (earthquake, tsunami, flood, volcano) by location: the Sea Ranch logic applied to a whole archipelago on the Ring of Fire.

ID · Business use

Banks and minimarket chains overlay population, income and competitor points to pick branch and ATM sites; city governments map flood-prone streets to position pumps and response teams.

Unit 03

Modeling considerations: geography, number, criteria

LO4

Figure 8.2 classifies location problems by geographic representation (plane or network), number of facilities (single or multiple) and optimization criteria (private or public).

Location on a plane

Infinite possible sites, identified by xy coordinates (or latitude and longitude). Distance is measured with a metric:

Euclidean: d = √[(xᵢ−xⱼ)² + (yᵢ−yⱼ)²]
Metropolitan: d = |xᵢ−xⱼ| + |yᵢ−yⱼ|

From (2,2) to (4,4): Euclidean 2.83, metropolitan 4.0.

Location on a network

Sites restricted to nodes (e.g., highway intersections); arcs carry shortest-route distance or time. More realistic for rivers with few bridges or mountains, but travel data are costly to collect. Both plane metrics need an average speed to convert distance to time.

Number of facilities and optimization criteria

Single vs. multiple

One site is mathematically easy. Multiple sites add assignment of demand to sites, capacities and, in health care, a service hierarchy (clinic → general hospital → specialist center).

Private sector

Minimize cost (distribution centers, services delivered to customers such as auditing or lawn care) or maximize profit when customers travel to the facility (retail).

Public sector

Benefits are hard to price, so surrogates are used: minimize average distance traveled (with a maximum-distance limit) or maximize demand created, traded off against investment.

Criteria change the answer. Abernathy and Hershey located one health center for three cities:

  • Maximize utilization (total visits) → city C, with many elderly residents for whom distance is a strong barrier.
  • Minimize distance per capita → city B, centrally located between the two larger cities.
  • Minimize distance per visit → city A, the largest city with the most mobile, frequent users.

Public criteria in Indonesia

Health policy aims for at least one puskesmas in every kecamatan: a coverage criterion. A regency deciding where to put one new hospital faces exactly the Abernathy–Hershey dilemma: the biggest town (most visits), the geographic center (shortest average distance), or the poorest, oldest district (most extra use created)?

Unit 04

Cross-median approach for a single facility

LO5

Where on Waikiki beach should a mat concession go to minimize the total walk of bathers? At the median of the bather distribution, with half the demand on each side, because the median minimizes the sum of absolute deviations. With the metropolitan metric the x and y directions separate, so on a plane the best site is where the x-median and y-median cross.

min Z = Σ wᵢ |xᵢ − xₛ| + Σ wᵢ |yᵢ − yₛ|

  1. Median = Σwᵢ / 2.
  2. Order points west→east and add weights until the sum reaches or exceeds the median: that point's x is an x-median. Repeat east→west.
  3. Do the same south→north and north→south for y.
  4. If the two passes give different values, any site between them is equally good: the answer can be a point, a line (a street) or an area (a block).
Example 8.1 · Copying service

Four office buildings: (1, 2) w 7; (2, 3) w 1; (3, 5) w 3; (4, 1) w 5 (hundreds of orders a month). Median = 16/2 = 8. x-medians: 2 and 3. y-median: 2. Any site on the segment from A(2, 2) to B(3, 2) gives the minimum total of 35.

Unit 05

The Huff model for a retail outlet

LO6

For a retail site the goal is profit, so we must estimate how much demand a store will capture against competitors. Huff's model borrows from gravity: attraction grows with a store's size and falls with travel time.

(8) Attraction

Aᵢⱼ = Sⱼ / Tᵢⱼ^λ

λ captures how much travel time deters a trip: about 2 for a shopping mall, 10 or more for convenience stores.

(9) Probability

Pᵢⱼ = Aᵢⱼ / Σⱼ Aᵢⱼ

The chance a customer in area i shops at store j, given all competing stores.

(10) Expenditure

Eⱼₖ = Σᵢ Pᵢⱼ Cᵢ Bᵢₖ

Customers × budget for product class k, weighted by probability.

(11) Market share

Mⱼₖ = Eⱼₖ / Σᵢ CᵢBᵢₖ

Then evaluate every site and store size for profit, and negotiate for the best.

Try it · Huff model calculator

Huff in an Indonesian city

Where traffic is heavy, travel time matters far more than kilometers: a mall 3 km away across a congested bridge can be "farther" than one 6 km away by toll road. That is why the model uses Tᵢⱼ as time. Supermarkets and hypermarkets also face λ differences: weekly shopping tolerates travel (low λ), while topping up instant noodles at night does not (high λ), which is why minimarkets win that trip.

Unit 06

Location set covering and regression

LO7

For public facilities, a common surrogate for benefit is the maximal service distance: how far the most distant customer must travel. Location set covering finds the minimum number of facilities, and their sites, so that every demand point is within that distance.

Example 8.3, rural medical clinics. Nine communities; every one must be within 30 miles of a clinic; community 6 can't host one. Steps:

  1. For each community, list the sites that can cover it within 30 miles (Table 8.10).
  2. Drop any set that contains a smaller set: the smaller sets are the binding ones, e.g., community 2 can only be covered by 1, 2 or 3.
  3. Pick sites common to several binding sets. Sites 3 and 8 cover everyone: clinic 3 serves 1–5, clinic 8 serves 6–9.

Solutions are often not unique: at 40 miles, (3, 8), (3, 9), (4, 7), (4, 8) and (4, 9) all work.

Regression analysis in location decisions

A chain with many sites can learn from them. La Quinta tested 35 candidate variables (competitive factors, demand generators, demographics, market awareness, physical attributes) to predict an inn's operating margin and found four that mattered:

Margin = 39.05 − 5.41·STATE + 5.86·PRICE − 3.09·INCOME + 1.75·COLLEGE

  • STATE (state population per inn): a low value means many inns, so strong brand awareness.
  • PRICE: the inn's room rate.
  • INCOME: average family income; negative because business travelers stay in non-residential areas.
  • COLLEGE: nearby college enrollment, a demand generator.

Candidate sites: X (up to 15,000), Y (up to 20,000), Z (up to 10,000). Net operating profit before taxes is 3.9%, 3.6% and 2.9% of sales for 10, 15 and 20 thousand sq ft.

Service benchmark · Here a bun, there a bun, everywhere a bun-bun

Au Bon Pain, Benetton and Starbucks embraced saturation: why advertise when customers can't walk a block without passing your shop? Close outlets are easier to supervise. It works best for non-destination shops in dense urban areas, and better for company-owned outlets, because cannibalization between franchisees hurts the owner who loses.

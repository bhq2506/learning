Service Management · Chapter 4

Every contact is a moment of truth.

A service is judged in brief encounters between a customer and a provider. This module walks through who controls those encounters, how firms prepare their people and customers for them, and why getting them right leads to profit.

Why it matters: Jan Carlzon and SAS

When Jan Carlzon led Scandinavian Airlines System (SAS), he reorganized the company around these encounters. He turned the organization chart upside down: frontline staff who deal with customers went to the top, and everyone else's job became serving them. SAS was split into profit centers down to the route level, and managers close to customers were given authority to decide on their own.

Unit 03

The service organization

LO3

The organization sets the stage for every encounter through its culture, how far it empowers staff, the control systems it uses, and the customer information it provides.

Culture

Shared beliefs guide decisions

Founders set norms and values. Disney calls staff "cast members" who work "onstage" or "backstage". FedEx tells stories of drivers going to extremes to keep the overnight promise.

Empowerment ≠ delegation

Trust, not permission

Empowerment trusts employees' own capacity to judge and act. Delegation is permission to act on someone's behalf, and it can be withdrawn.

Inverted-T structure

Fewer supervisors, better-informed front line

Frontline staff get training, motivation and timely information. Middle managers become facilitators. Examples: Taco Bell, ServiceMaster, Marriott.

Four commitments of the empowerment model:

  1. Invest in people as much as, or more than, in machines.
  2. Use technology to support contact personnel, not to monitor or replace them.
  3. Treat recruiting and training of contact personnel as critical to success.
  4. Link pay to performance at all levels.

Information that lets the front line act

Unit 04

Contact personnel

LO4LO6

Good contact staff are flexible, tolerate ambiguity, read situational cues and show empathy. Empathy matters more than age, education, sales knowledge, sales training or intelligence.

Selection: three interview techniques

No reliable test measures service orientation, so firms rely on interviews.

Abstract questioning

Open-ended, about past experience

"What type of customer was most difficult for you, and why? How did you handle them?"

Reveals: attention to detail, whether the applicant gathers information, interpersonal style, willingness to adapt.

Watch for: "puffery"; reflecting well on the past does not guarantee performing well on the job.

Situational vignette

A specific scenario: "What would you do?"

A timid customer returns stale cakes demanding a refund, but your chef clearly didn't make them.

Reveals: instincts, common sense, judgment, whether they can think on their feet.

Watch for: good talkers who lack a real desire to serve.

Role playing

A simulated encounter with "actors"

Usually used in the final recruiting phase. The interviewer can change the situation as it unfolds.

Reveals: behavior under stress, in the applicant's own words. The most realistic of the three.

Watch for: hard to compare applicants directly; needs scripting and rehearsal.

Training for difficult encounters

About 75 percent of reported communication difficulties do not come from a technical breakdown in delivery. They come from customers with unrealistic expectations. Training should cover both kinds of challenge in Table 4.3, using prepared scripts and role play.

Problem customers (unrealistic expectations)
  1. Unreasonable demands: "I want to carry all my luggage on board."
  2. Abusive or hostile attitude: verbal or physical abuse of staff.
  3. Inappropriate behavior: e.g. intoxicated passengers.
  4. Unanticipated demands: "My wife is in labor."
  5. Demands contrary to policies: "Our party of 10 wants separate checks."
Service failure
  1. Unavailable service: "I reserved a table by the window."
  2. Slow performance: "We've waited an hour and no one took our order."
  3. Unacceptable service: "There's a hair in my soup!"

Failures are also a chance to show flexibility in recovery.

Example script

To "I want to carry all my luggage on board": "I'm very sorry, but federal safety regulations permit only two carry-on pieces that fit under the seat or overhead. May I check your larger pieces all the way to your final destination?"

Unit 05

The customer

LO5LO7

For the customer every purchase matters; for the provider it is routine. That gap is why bored staff struggle to match a customer's emotional involvement.

Four customer groups

Economizing

Maximum value for time, effort, money

Demanding and sometimes fickle. Losing them is an early warning of competitive threats.

Ethical

Supports socially responsible firms

Loyal to firms with a community reputation, e.g. Ronald McDonald House.

Personalizing

Wants recognition and conversation

Greeting by first name; customer files can recreate this at scale.

Convenience

No interest in shopping around

Often pays extra for hassle-free service, e.g. Amazon Prime.

Practice · Which customer is this?

Choosing self-service vs. full service

Customers weigh seven dimensions: time, control of the situation, efficiency, human contact, risk, effort, and dependence on others. Those who chose self-service rated control as the most important. Cost-leadership services can use this by making customers coproducers.

Scripts in coproduction

Customer as partial employee

Customers follow scripts set by social norms or by the service design: carrying a cafeteria tray, serving themselves at a salad bar, busing the table. Scripts let each side predict the other's behavior, which gives both a sense of control. If a customer breaks the script (leaving the table uncleared), an employee must do the task.

Learning a new script

New technology needs a new script. Self-checkout first needs an attendant to coach customers; once they learn it, the attendant may no longer be needed. Scripts are easier to learn when they extend past behavior. Windows succeeded partly because all applications share one interface, so there is only one script to learn.

Customer behavior after COVID-19

Habits that may last

Grocery and meal delivery, ordering by smartphone to skip wait staff, and higher expectations for ventilation and sanitation.

Work from home

Less demand for downtown offices and nearby eateries.

Remote meetings

Business travel may never fully recover, which hurts airlines, hotels and convention centers.

Service benchmark: Miss Manners on complaint handling

A complaint exchange has two roles: the outraged one and the "calm down, it happens" one. Whoever receives the complaint gets to pick a role, and the complainer is left with the other.

If staff take the outraged role (a sincere apology plus a promise to act), the customer tends to shift toward "it's not that important." The apology and the promise are the ingredients; the tone is what makes it work.

Unit 07

Apply it: cases and discussion

LO2LO3LO4

Case 4.1 · Amy's Ice Cream (Austin, Texas)

Ice cream is ice cream, so Amy's competes on service: "ice cream theater" put on by outgoing, creative scoopers. Applicants get a plain white paper bag as the application form. Someone who only writes their name and address on it is probably less entertaining than someone who turns it into a puppet.

Pay is low (thin margins, mostly part-time), yet Amy's attracts strong staff because it is freedom-oriented rather than rules-oriented: any hat, your own music, a say in store art, and everyone does every job, including managers.

Questions

  1. Describe the service culture at Amy's.
  2. What personality attributes does Amy's look for?
  3. Design a selection procedure using abstract questioning, a situational vignette and/or role playing.

Case 4.2 · Enterprise Rent-A-Car

Enterprise positions its "greatest idea" as personal service and focuses on the local (replacement and discretionary) rental market, picking customers up and dropping them off.

It hires almost only college graduates, favoring "people people" such as athletes and social directors. Promotion comes from within, and pay is tied to branch profit, which is shared openly.

Link to the chapter: selection, culture and a profit-linked reward system are all links in the service profit chain.

Topics for discussion

  1. How can we design for self-recovery when a self-service failure occurs?
  2. What are the organizational and marketing implications of treating the customer as a "partial employee"?
  3. How does perceived control differ between one-on-one service and group service?
  4. How does a service script relate to service quality?
  5. If customer roles are set by cultural norms, how can services be exported?

Group exercise: in small groups, find a real example of each control system: belief, boundary, diagnostic and interactive.

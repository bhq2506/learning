Service Management · Chapter 3

A great service starts as a design, not a guess.

Designing a service system means choosing its location, facility, procedures, quality measures, customer role, equipment and capacity, and then refining it after launch. This module covers where new services come from, how they are developed and drawn, and four ways to design the delivery system.

Start studyingOpen the blueprint lab

The design is the strategy: FedEx

Frederick Smith's college paper on guaranteed overnight air freight reportedly got a "C". His insight, borrowed from electrical networks, was a hub-and-spoke system: every package flies to Memphis at night, is sorted in about two hours, and flies out for next-morning delivery. Even Los Angeles to San Diego went via Memphis.

Same idea, Indonesia

Express couriers such as JNE, J&T and SiCepat also run hub-and-spoke networks: parcels are consolidated at regional sorting hubs, then line-hauled to destination hubs overnight. The network design, not just the trucks, makes "next-day" possible across an archipelago.

Unit 01

Sources of service sector growth

LO1

Three forces fuel service growth: information technology, innovation, and changing demographics. In the U.S., information (digital) services contribute about 53% of GDP and are growing at the expense of physical products (Karmarkar & Apte).

Information technology

Proximity is no longer required

Smartphones make banking an electronic service; X-rays can be read by a radiologist offshore. IT creates new value chains and room for new intermediaries.

The Internet as enabler

A network of networks

The Internet is the physical network; the Web is the hyperlinked documents running on it (HTTP). Mobile data and social media let firms push information to customers ("your flight is delayed") instead of waiting for inquiries.

Karmarkar & Apte's propositions

Information chains

(1) Most GDP will come from information chains rather than supply chains. (2) Managing them is about process economics. (3) Technology drives those economics.

Example 3.1 · Impact of 5G

5G uses higher-frequency radio waves in very small cells, giving up to 10 Gbps and serving laptops and IoT, not just phones. It enables VR fitting rooms, remote tellers, AR sports viewing, remote patient monitoring and vehicle-to-vehicle communication.

Drawbacks: towers must be close together "like street lamps", so rollout is costly and urban-first; obstructions hurt signal; batteries drain faster.

5G in Indonesia

Telkomsel launched Indonesia's first commercial 5G network on 27 May 2021, starting in a handful of cities. Coverage remains concentrated in urban areas, which is exactly the rollout pattern the textbook predicts from 5G's short range and tower cost.

Think: which Surabaya services would change first with reliable 5G: hospitals, ports, or campus labs?

Where innovations come from

Push theory

Technology looking for a problem. 3M's weak adhesive became Post-it notes; the DVD spawned video rental, then Netflix by mail, then streaming.

Pull theory

A market need pulls the innovation. Merrill Lynch's cash management account answered investors' demand for higher interest in the 1980s.

Other sources

Unexpected events (security-evacuation insurance after the Arab Spring), observant frontline staff (a concierge noticing taxi demand → airport shuttle), and information from other activities (auto-parts sales revealing failure patterns).

Changing demographics

After the French Revolution, Paris went from two restaurants to over 500 as nobles' chefs lost their jobs. An aging U.S. population drives health care, financial planning and leisure travel.

Indonesia's demographic shift

Indonesia has a large productive-age population (the "demographic bonus") alongside a growing elderly share. The first fuels demand for digital payments, ride-hailing, online learning and entertainment; the second for home care, chronic-disease services and pension and insurance products.

Unit 02

Innovation in services and adopting technology

LO2LO3

The U.S. National Science Foundation separates basic research (knowledge without specific application), applied research (knowledge for a specific need) and development (systematic use of knowledge to create a product, service or method). Service innovation is mostly applied R&D; non-manufacturing firms did about 31% of U.S. industry R&D in 2013.

Why service innovation is different

Hard to protect

Service systems are transparent and easy to imitate; patents are difficult to obtain.

Incremental

Customers participate, so change must be evolutionary to be accepted.

Integration

People, products and technology must be integrated as a system.

Hard to prototype

Few services can be tested in a lab. Burger King built a mock outlet in a Miami warehouse to test drive-throughs and breakfast.

Challenges of adopting new technology

"The process is the product"

Front-office technology succeeds only if customers accept it. They may lose personal attention, need new skills (ATM, self-pump fuel) or give up a benefit (float lost with electronic transfer). Employees, as internal customers, need retraining too. Use focus groups to anticipate problems, e.g., the need for cameras at ATMs.

Back office: everyone must agree

MICR check codes made check clearing efficient only once all U.S. banks used the same imprint; uncooperative banks' checks still had to be sorted by hand. Bank of America led, but self-interest moved the banks: volume had outgrown manual sorting.

The MICR lesson in Indonesia: QRIS

Before 2019, every e-wallet and bank had its own QR code, so a warung needed a stack of stickers. Bank Indonesia launched QRIS (Quick Response Code Indonesian Standard) on 17 August 2019 and made it mandatory for QR payment providers from 1 January 2020. One standard code let any app pay any merchant: the same "everyone must agree" logic as MICR, imposed by the regulator instead of negotiated among banks.

Readiness to embrace new technology

Technology readiness is a person's propensity to embrace new technology. Research found eight paradoxes: technology triggers both positive and negative feelings. Managers should assess customers' readiness (to set the pace and support needed) and employees' readiness (staff with high interpersonal skill and high readiness make good tech-support people).

Try it · Where do you stand on the eight paradoxes?

Think about a technology you use daily, such as mobile banking. Slide toward the feeling that is stronger for you. This is a reflection tool, not the validated Technology Readiness Index.

Blockchain: a radical innovation

A blockchain is a secure, distributed ledger with no central computer to hack. Each block holds a timestamp, data, and the hash of the previous block. A hash is a one-way function that maps any data to a fixed-length string. Change one block and its hash changes, breaking every link after it. Trust comes from mass collaboration and code, not from banks or governments. Uses: smart contracts that self-execute, and faster settlement (Bank of America/Merrill Lynch with Microsoft). Owners sign with a private key; others verify with the matching public key.

Try it · Tamper with a land-title chain (illustrative)

Buying land involves many parties (seller, buyer, banks, notary/PPAT, BPN), each with separate records. Edit any block's data and watch the chain break. Re-sealing is easy here; on a real public chain, an attacker would have to redo every later block faster than thousands of honest computers.

Example 3.2 · Virtual health

Telehealth

Synchronous (live video visits), asynchronous (store-and-forward: a photo of a rash, a scan sent to a specialist), and remote patient monitoring.

Digital therapeutics

Replacement therapies (software treatments in place of drugs) and treatment optimization (medication adherence, side-effect tracking).

Care navigation

Patient self-directed care (24-hour access to your own records) and e-triage (finding and booking the right care by symptoms, price and quality).

Virtual health in Indonesia

Telemedicine apps such as Halodoc and Alodokter grew rapidly during COVID-19 (synchronous telehealth). BPJS Kesehatan's Mobile JKN app lets members take an online queue number at their health facility (care navigation), and the Ministry of Health's SATUSEHAT platform integrates health records across providers (self-directed care and data exchange).

The new service development process and service design elements

LO5

Ideas come from customers (menu suggestions), frontline staff acting as "listening posts", mined customer databases, demographic trends and technology. They feed a four-stage cycle around a service product made of people, systems and technology. Tap a stage.

People

Employees must be recruited, trained and empowered (Barnes & Noble staff can check books in print). Customers' roles must be defined and motivated (self-check-in at Hyatt Regency).

Systems and technology

Back-office systems (a clinic's patient records) support staff; front-office systems (online hotel booking) face customers. Firms must monitor technology: a first mover like Amazon can build a loyal base.

Service design elements

Like an architect's drawings for foundation, structure, plumbing and electrical systems, a service concept is engineered through structural elements (delivery system, facility design, location, capacity planning) and managerial elements (information, quality, service encounter, capacity and demand management). Together they tell customers and staff what to expect. Shouldice Hospital in Toronto, which repairs only inguinal hernias, shows every element serving one strategy.

Unit 04

Service blueprinting

LO6

A blueprint captures the service process on paper so the concept can be tested before any commitment, avoiding costly trial and error. It has five rows separated by three lines.

Build order

1. Customer actions first: they drive the flow left to right. 2. Physical evidence above each action. 3. Onstage contact. 4. Backstage contact. 5. Support processes. 6. Mark fail points.

The three lines

Line of interaction (dashed): direct customer–staff contact, drawn as two-way arrows. Line of visibility (dotted): the customer can't see below it. Line of internal interaction (dashed): staff vs. support processes.

Questions to ask

Is the evidence consistent with expectations? Can customer actions be eliminated or made self-service? Do onstage roles need different skills? Is backstage staffed to avoid onstage delays? How do we pre-empt each fail point?

Unit 05

Strategic positioning: complexity and divergence

LO4
Complexity

Number and intricacy of steps

A fast-food take-out order is less complex than a gourmet French dinner. Low complexity = focus/specialization (shops selling only cookies, coffee or ice cream; must be unique or very high quality). High complexity = adding services to raise revenue per customer (supermarkets becoming superstores with pharmacy, bank, flowers).

Divergence

Freedom to customize at each step

An attorney's work is highly divergent; a paralegal's is not. Low divergence = standardized, narrowly skilled staff, consistent quality at low cost (H&R Block's standard tax returns). High divergence = niche strategy for customers who pay for personalization (men's hair-styling salons).

Positioning in Indonesia

Low complexity: single-product kiosks (es teh, martabak, grab-and-go coffee). High complexity: minimarkets that add bill payment, tickets, parcel and ATM services. Low divergence: a standardized pangkas rambut cut in 15 minutes. High divergence: a premium barbershop with consultation, styling and grooming.

The textbook repositions a family restaurant by changing steps up or down. Try it on a Padang restaurant, starting from the classic hidang service (all dishes brought to the table; you pay only for what you eat).

Unit 06

A taxonomy for service process design

LO4

Services can be classified on three dimensions: divergence (standardized vs. customized), the object of the process (goods, information or people) and the type of customer contact (none, indirect, or direct, which is either self-service or interaction with a worker).

Divergence

Standardized: high volume, routine tasks, low skills, room for automation (vending, car wash), consistency by limiting discretion. Customized: judgment, information exchange, high technical and analytic skills, empowered workers (counseling, landscaping).

Object

Goods: customer's property must be secured (auto repair); facilitating goods need stock and quality control (McDonald's purchasing). Information: back office (check processing) or direct (counseling). People: physical or geographic change; needs interpersonal skills, facility design and location.

Contact

Direct: full sensory awareness; manage queues and image. Indirect: via the internet or phone. None: decoupled, so run for efficiency like manufacturing (dry cleaning = batch; auto repair = job shop). A bank does all three: loan interview, automatic payments, back-office records.

Unit 07

Four generic approaches to service system design

LO7

The service package (supporting facility, facilitating goods, information, explicit and implicit services) defines the system. Four approaches can design it, and they can be combined: a bank isolates check processing, uses self-service ATMs and gives personal loan service.

Customer contact: efficiency vs. sales opportunity (Fig. 3.6)

Customer contact is the share of total service time the customer is present. Low-contact work can be sealed off and run as a factory (an airline's maintenance depot, a hospital laundry). The trade-off: more contact means less efficiency but more chance for add-on sales. Choose a bank channel.

Unit 08

Intellectual property

LO8

Intellectual property rights are exclusive rights over creations of the mind used in commerce. They let owners earn temporary monopoly profits, rewarding creativity and recovering R&D costs. A service firm protects its brand by defending them: McDonald's sued a competitor for using its golden arches, because a customer who got bad service there might blame McDonald's.

Patent

Industrial property: an invention for commercial use, protected for a set period (an artificial heart).

Trademark

A distinctive sign that prevents confusion in the market (McDonald's golden arches).

Industrial design

Protects appearance, style or design (Starbucks' store ambience).

Trade secret

Proprietary business knowledge or practices (KFC's batter recipe).

IP protection in Indonesia

Rights are registered with the Directorate General of Intellectual Property (DJKI) under the Ministry of Law. Key laws: UU 13/2016 on patents, UU 20/2016 on trademarks and geographical indications, UU 31/2000 on industrial designs, UU 30/2000 on trade secrets, and UU 28/2014 on copyright. Indonesia's trademark system is first-to-file: a food or café brand that grows popular before registering its name risks someone else registering it first. Register early.

Unit 09

Key terms and cases

Case 3.1 · 100 Yen Sushi House (Tokyo)

Cooks inside an oval counter put sushi plates on a conveyor; every plate costs 100 yen (cheap items get four pieces, expensive ones two). The cashier counts plates instead of using a register. Demand data tell the owner what to make and when; fish arrives several times a day just in time; work passes hand to hand; plates left unsold for about 30 minutes are removed.

Questions: (1) Prepare a blueprint. (2) What differentiates it and what advantages result? (3) How is just-in-time used?

A Padang restaurant does the same trick

In hidang service, many small plates arrive at once; you eat what you want and staff count the plates (and pieces) touched to compute the bill, much like counting sushi plates. Food is cooked in batches ahead of demand, portions are standardized per plate, and untouched plates return to the display. Compare: where is the "conveyor", where is the just-in-time, and where is the hygiene risk?

Case 3.2 · Commuter Cleaning

A proposed dry-cleaning service for suburban commuters: drop off and pick up at park-and-ride lots and train stations, using a FedEx-style hub-and-spoke system. Start with business shirts only; decide same-day vs. next-day; consider outsourcing the cleaning plant.

Indonesian adaptation: how would you design it around KRL Commuter Line stations in Jabodetabek, or the Surabaya–Sidoarjo commuter route? Who owns the station space, and how would you link it to a laundry-kiloan back office?

Case 3.3 · Zoom Video Communications

A video-conferencing service whose use exploded during the COVID-19 pandemic. Use it to discuss radical vs. incremental innovation, technology readiness, information empowerment and the risks of rapid scaling (security and privacy).

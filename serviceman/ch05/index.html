Service Management · Chapter 5

The building is part of the service.

A facility shapes how customers and staff feel and behave, and its layout decides how fast work flows. This module covers servicescapes, facility design, and the tools for finding a process bottleneck and improving a layout.

Unit 01

Orientation and servicescapes

LO1LO2

The first thing anyone needs on entering a place is orientation: "Where am I?" (place) and "How does this work, and what do I do next?" (function). Without cues, customers feel anxious and helpless.

Previous experience

Formula facilities remove disorientation. Holiday Inn promised "no surprises" at any location.

Design legibility

Let customers see into and through the space. A Hyatt atrium shows the whole hotel at a glance and lets guests take cues from others.

Orientation aids (wayfinding)

"You Are Here" maps aligned with the viewer, landmarks such as plants and art, color-coded subway lines.

Unit 02

Facility design

LO3

Design and layout are the supporting-facility part of the service package. Shouldice Hospital groups operating rooms so surgeons can consult, serves meals only in shared dining rooms so patients walk, and leaves TVs out of rooms so they don't lie around.

1 · Nature & objectives

The core service sets the parameters: a fire station must house trucks and 24-hour shifts; a clinic needs privacy. Design should also be appropriate: would you bank at a trailer?

2 · Land & space

Cost, zoning and area. In cities, build upward (McDonald's second-floor loft in Copenhagen). Plan parking and room to expand.

3 · Flexibility

"Designing for the future": drive-throughs added to walk-in restaurants; airports built before hub-and-spoke. Chipotle's "Chipotlanes" and ghost kitchens during COVID-19.

4 · Security

Airport screening after 9/11, surveillance cameras, pool fences, bollards and anti-shoplifting tags.

5 · Esthetics

Nordstrom's carpet, space and three-way mirrors vs. an outlet store's piled tables. Esthetics shape both customer attitudes and employee service.

6 · Community & environment

Parking for neighbors, kennel noise and odor, a dry cleaner's chemicals. Zoning and public groups guide acceptance.

The office after COVID-19

Pass-protected entrance, distanced desks with partitions, touch-screen walls so remote members join meetings, "virtual porches" for chance encounters, possibly VR, and HEPA ventilation nobody notices.

Unit 05

Flow process layout: the work allocation problem

LO6

In a flow (product) layout every customer follows the same sequence, like a cafeteria line. The job that takes longest per customer is the bottleneck. To raise capacity: add a worker there, give that job an aid, or regroup tasks so jobs take nearly equal time.

Example 5.3 · Driver's license renewal

The office must reach 120 applicants per hour with only one extra clerk (7 staff in total). Activity 1 comes first; activity 6 is last and must be done by a uniformed officer. The office has one eye-test machine and one expensive camera. Assign activities to jobs and set clerks per job.

Activities and job assignment

Jobs

Why the easy fix fails

Adding a clerk to activity 3 doubles its rate to 120/hr, but the bottleneck just shifts to activity 4 (eye test, 40 s = 90/hr).

Reengineering

Train each clerk to do activities 1–5 (165 s, about 22 per hour each). Customers deal with one clerk, wait once, and repeat nothing, and staffing can follow demand. It needs a workstation, eye tester and camera for each clerk.

Unit 06

Job shop process layout: the relative location problem

LO7

In a job shop layout there is no fixed sequence, so service can be customized (law, medicine, a university campus). Each department needs a waiting area. The question is where to put departments relative to each other.

Why a heuristic

n departments in n locations give n! layouts. With 10 departments that is 3,628,800, too many to check. Operations sequence analysis improves a layout step by step instead.

Other objectives

Minimizing travel isn't always the goal. A casino routes guests past shops and slot machines on the way to elevators and restaurants.

Operations sequence analysis in four steps

  1. Triangularize the flow matrix: add flows in both directions for each pair.
  2. Start with an initial layout on a grid (judgment or experience).
  3. For each nonadjacent pair, multiply flow by the number of grid squares between them (diagonals count as adjacent). Sum these.
  4. Swap or move departments to put high-flow pairs next to each other; keep the change if the total drops. Stop when no swap helps.

Ocean World theme park: 124 → 96 → 70 → 58 after moves that placed high-flow attractions side by side and kept the site rectangular.

Unit 07

Key terms, benchmark and cases

Service benchmark · Where, oh where shall we go?

Two travelers rebooked from Delta to American land at Dallas–Fort Worth needing a Delta gate. Monitors and maps show only American flights and concourses, a tram station is closed, and staff can't help. A transfer that takes under 10 minutes takes over an hour.

Poor signage causes bad tempers, missed flights and even accidents. It is a wayfinding failure.

Cases for class

  • 5.1 HMO (A): lay out a clinic to minimize walking, with acutely ill patients kept apart from well patients and radiography not next to the main waiting room.
  • 5.2 HMO (B): long pharmacy waits at peak hours; rebalance the work of five staff.
  • 5.3 Esquire Department Store and 5.4 Central Market: layout and flow in retail.

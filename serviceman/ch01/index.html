Service Management · Chapter 1

Most of the world now works in services.

We are living through the greatest labor migration since the industrial revolution: from farms and factories into services. This module explains why services sit at the heart of every economy, how economies evolve, what makes a service different from a product, and two tools to describe and classify any service: the service package and the service process matrix.

Indonesia holds 3.9% of the world's labor force, the fifth largest. The textbook shows 32% in agriculture, 21% in industry and 47% in services. Compare the U.S. at 0.7% / 20.3% / 79.1%.

Unit 01

What a service is, and why economies depend on it

LO1

Every definition of service shares two themes: intangibility and simultaneous consumption. Services are not "what is left over after farming and manufacturing"; they are the activities that let farming and manufacturing work at all.

Three definitions worth knowing

Zeithaml, Bitner & Gremler

Deeds, processes, performances

A service is something done, not something owned.

Wirtz & Lovelock

Access without ownership

Time-based performances that bring desired results to people or their possessions. Customers pay for access to goods, labor, skills, facilities, networks and systems, but do not take ownership of the physical elements.

Spohrer et al.

A service system

A value-coproduction configuration of people, technology, other service systems and shared information (language, processes, metrics, prices, policies, laws).

The facilitating role of services (Figure 1.1)

Services are at the center of the economy, not at the edge. Picture manufacturing, extractive industries and households as islands; services are the bridges, ports and power lines that connect them.

Infrastructure services

Transportation, communications, utilities, banking. The foundation: no economy industrializes without them.

Distribution services

Wholesale and retail trade. With infrastructure, they are the channel of distribution to the final consumer.

Business services

Advertising, consulting, accounting, IT. Specialists supply them more cheaply than a manufacturer could in-house.

Personal services

Restaurants, lodging, child care: former household tasks moved into the market economy. Self-service (airline kiosks, online brokerage) also counts.

Government services

Education, health care, roads, clean water, public safety: the stable environment investment needs.

Servitization

Manufacturers deliberately couple services with products: car makers profit from financing and leasing; Otis Elevator's maintenance contracts far exceed its equipment profits.

ID · Servitization

Buying a motorbike "on credit"

Most Indonesian motorbikes are bought through financing. Astra's financing arm FIF lends for Honda motorbikes, and AHASS (Astra Honda Authorized Service Station) workshops sell the after-sales service. The bike is the hook; financing and service are recurring revenue.

ID · Infrastructure

Why logistics costs matter

Across an archipelago, ports, inter-island shipping, couriers and payment systems decide whether a Sulawesi cacao farmer or a Surabaya furniture maker can reach buyers at all. Infrastructure services come before industrialization.

ID · Personal services

Household work goes to market

Laundry kiloan, catering rantangan, home-cleaning apps and daycare near office districts: tasks once done at home are now bought as services, especially in dual-income urban households.

Unit 02

Five stages of economic activity

LO2

The Clark-Fisher hypothesis: as productivity (output per labor-hour) rises in one sector, labor moves into the next. Economies can be classified by what the majority of their workforce does. The textbook splits Clark's single "tertiary" stage into three, giving five stages.

In the U.S., 3 in 10 workers were in services in the early 1900s, 5 in 10 by 1950 and about 8 in 10 today. The decline of agriculture is almost a mirror image of the rise of services, and the same trajectory repeats in every nation in Table 1.1. Competition in services is also global (Indian call centers, British financial services), though many countries protect domestic firms; India and Mexico, for instance, prohibited foreign companies from selling insurance.

Indonesia's own Clark-Fisher story

World Bank estimates put roughly 45% of Indonesian workers in agriculture around 2000. The textbook's figure is 32%, and BPS's August 2025 survey shows 28.2% in agriculture and 13.9% in manufacturing. Indonesia skipped part of the "factory phase": many workers left farms directly for trade, food service, transport and, more recently, platform work such as ojek online drivers and online sellers.

Think: is moving from farm to ride-hailing the same "upgrade" as moving from farm to factory? What happens to productivity and job security?

Unit 03

Preindustrial, industrial and postindustrial societies

LO3

Sociologist Daniel Bell describes three kinds of society by the "game" people play. In a preindustrial society life is a game against nature; in an industrial society, a game against fabricated nature; in a postindustrial society, a game among persons.

How the transition happens

Step 1

Services such as transportation and utilities develop to support industry. Labor-saving machines push workers into maintenance and repair.

Step 2

Population growth and mass consumption expand wholesale and retail trade, banking, real estate and insurance.

Step 3 · Engel's law

As income rises, the share spent on food and the home falls; spending moves to durables and then to services (like Maslow's hierarchy). Health and education become the condition for the "good life".

The nature of the service sector today

Not just low-wage jobs

About 31% of U.S. employment in 2019 was in high-skill services: professional and business services, health care and social assistance, education. In 1956 white-collar workers first outnumbered blue-collar workers in the U.S.; services now produce about 70% of U.S. national income.

Recession-resistant, usually

Services cannot be inventoried, so demand is steadier; in recessions people defer buying goods and repair what they have, creating maintenance jobs. COVID-19 was different: distancing rules created a service-dominated recession, while manufacturers kept producing for inventory.

ID · Three societies at once

Indonesia contains all three: rice farmers in rural Java and NTT, factory belts in Cikarang, Gresik and Batam, and knowledge workers in Jakarta's business districts or Surabaya's tech offices. National averages hide this dual (even triple) economy.

ID · COVID-19 hit services hardest

Bali, heavily dependent on tourism, saw international arrivals fall to almost zero in 2021. Hotels, restaurants, drivers and guides lost income while manufacturing and agriculture kept running. Exactly the "service-dominated recession" pattern.

ID · Engel's law at home

As household income rises, notice where extra rupiah go: from rice and cooking oil toward mobile data, eating out, tuition (bimbel), health insurance and holidays. That is the demand base for the services you will manage.

Unit 05

Service-dominant logic

LO5

Service-dominant (S-D) logic is a service-centered alternative to the goods-centered view of exchange. Service (singular) is the application of competencies, knowledge and skills, for the benefit of another. Goods may be involved, but what matters is value-in-use: value as realized and determined by the customer.

Goods-dominant logic

Value is made in the factory and embedded in the product (value-in-exchange). The customer is a target; the firm delivers value.

Service-dominant logic

Value emerges when the customer uses the offering (value-in-use). The customer co-creates value; the firm can only offer value propositions.

The 10 foundational premises (Table 1.5)

Open each premise for the explanation and an Indonesian illustration.

SSME: service science

S-D logic underpins service science, management, and engineering (SSME), championed by IBM's Almaden Research Center: applying scientific, management and engineering disciplines to work one organization performs for and with another, to make productivity, quality, compliance, growth and learning more predictable in coproduction relationships. Its heart is the transfer and sharing of resources within and among service systems.

Unit 06

Six distinctive characteristics of service operations

LO6

In services, inputs are the customers themselves; resources are facilitating goods, employee labor and capital. Customers arrive when they choose, with unique demands, so matching capacity to demand is the core challenge. The six characteristics are interrelated.

1 · Customer participation

The customer is present, so the facility must be designed from the customer's view (decor, noise, layout). Customers act as "temporary employees": fast-food diners order and clear tables, so capacity rises with demand. The customer's knowledge, motivation and honesty affect performance.

2 · Simultaneity

Produced and consumed at once, so no inventory buffer. Services are open systems: demand variation hits the operation directly. Customer waiting (queuing) replaces inventory, and there's no chance to inspect before delivery.

3 · Perishability

An empty seat, an unused room, a dentist's idle hour are lost forever. Options: smooth demand, adjust capacity, or let customers wait.

4 · Intangibility

Ideas can't be patented, so firms expand fast (often by franchising) to pre-empt imitators. Customers can't test before buying; they rely on reputation, and governments license and regulate (engineers, doctors, utilities), possibly at the cost of competition.

5 · Heterogeneity

Intangibility plus customer participation means variation from customer to customer. Standards and training create consistency; customer feedback helps control quality. A disgruntled employee can do irreparable harm: "happy guests need happy employees".

6 · Nontransferrable ownership

Customers buy access to, or rental of, resources for a time, often shared concurrently with other customers (Table 1.6 below).

ID · Perishability

Mudik and the empty return train

Before Lebaran, trains and buses out of Jakarta and Surabaya sell out weeks ahead (KAI opens sales well before the holiday), while the return direction runs half empty. After Lebaran the flows reverse. Capacity cannot be stored for the peak.

ID · Intangibility

Licences as a quality signal

Doctors need an STR and practice licence, notaries are appointed by the ministry, and banks are supervised by OJK. Customers can't "test" these services first, so the state guarantees minimum competence.

ID · Heterogeneity

The same app, a different ride

Ride-hailing apps standardize price, routing and payment, yet your trip still depends on the driver. Ratings are customers doing quality control, the textbook's point about feedback.

Unit 07

The service package

LO7

Managers struggle to describe their "product". The service package is a bundle of goods and services with information, provided in some environment. Its five features form an onion with the service experience at the core, and every layer shapes the customer's perception.

  1. Supporting facility: physical resources that must be in place first (golf course, hospital, airplane).
  2. Facilitating goods: material purchased or consumed, or supplied by the customer (food, spare parts, legal documents).
  3. Information: data from the customer or provider that enable efficient, customized service (medical records, seat availability, GPS location to dispatch a taxi).
  4. Explicit services: benefits readily observable by the senses; the essential features (no pain after a filling, a smooth-running car).
  5. Implicit services: psychological benefits sensed vaguely; extrinsic features (status of a degree, privacy of a loan office, worry-free repair).

Consistency matters. In a budget hotel, the supporting facility is a plain concrete building, facilitating goods are minimal (soap, towels), information books the room, the explicit service is a clean, comfortable bed and the implicit services are a friendly clerk and a well-lit parking area. Adding bellhops would destroy the bargain image. The weight of facilitating goods also places a service on a continuum from "pure" (counseling) to mixed (car maintenance).

Two Indonesian packages side by side

Rumah Makan Padang
Facility: shop with stacked-dish window display. Goods: rendang, rice, sambal, plates served hidang. Information: staff count plates touched to compute the bill. Explicit: a fast, filling, flavorful meal. Implicit: abundance and generosity, a taste of home for Minang diners.

Puskesmas
Facility: clinic building, waiting room, lab. Goods: medicines, vaccines, forms. Information: JKN membership, patient records, queue number. Explicit: diagnosis and treatment, immunization. Implicit: reassurance, respectful treatment, privacy in the consultation room.

Unit 08

The service process matrix and the open-systems view

LO8LO6

Roger Schmenner's matrix shows that management problems are shared across industries. It classifies services on two dimensions: degree of labor intensity (ratio of labor cost to capital cost) and degree of interaction and customization. Hospitals can learn housekeeping from hotels; dry cleaners can borrow night-deposit boxes from banks.

Try it · Place Indonesian services in the matrix

Challenges for managers (Figure 1.8)

Low labor intensity (factory, shop)

Capital decisions; keeping up with technological advances; managing demand to avoid peaks and promote off-peak use; scheduling service delivery.

High labor intensity (mass, professional)

Hiring; training; methods development and control; employee welfare; scheduling the workforce; controlling far-flung locations; managing growth.

Low interaction / customization (factory, mass)

Marketing; making the service "warm"; attention to physical surroundings; managing a fairly rigid hierarchy that needs standard operating procedures.

High interaction / customization (shop, professional)

Fighting cost increases; maintaining quality; reacting to consumer intervention in the process; managing the advancement of people; managing a flat hierarchy with loose superior–subordinate relationships; gaining employee loyalty.

Open-systems view

A factory is a closed system: finished-goods inventory decouples it from customers, like coffee in a sealed thermos. A service is an open system: the customer is an input transformed by the service process into an output with some degree of satisfaction, like an uncovered cup of coffee interacting with the air.

So production and marketing cannot be separated. Marketing (1) educates customers to play their role and (2) smooths demand to match capacity. "The process is the product": multiple performance measures, trained and empowered staff, and attention to the total experience, from facility design to diversions in waiting areas.

Service benchmark · sharing economy

Uber and Airbnb

Internet, GPS and smartphones let people share their physical assets for income. Benefits: savings for customers, extra income and flexibility for providers, convenient access; for the economy, fewer new products sold, less pressure on resources and emissions.

Sharing economy, Indonesia

Gojek began in 2010 as a call center for ojek drivers and launched its app in 2015, competing with Grab. Uber sold its Southeast Asian business to Grab in 2018. Homestays listed on booking apps let families in Yogyakarta or Labuan Bajo earn from spare rooms. Drivers are part of the open system: their mood, vehicle and route are the service.

Unit 09

Key terms and cases

Case 1.1 · Village Volvo

Two former Volvo-dealer mechanics (22 years' combined experience) repair out-of-warranty Volvos at a reasonable cost in a prefabricated building with four bays. Clients book appointments and discuss the problem directly with their mechanic, sometimes on a test drive; a Custom Care Vehicle Dossier keeps each car's history; the service manager gives estimates and calls before any extra work. Quick jobs use fixed drop-in hours; no repairs are done during the 7–8 AM and 5–6 PM contact peaks. There is no alternate transportation yet; the owners are considering Uber or Lyft credits.

Questions: (1) Describe the service package. (2) How are the distinctive service characteristics illustrated? (3) How could the back office (repairs) be run like a factory? (4) How can it differentiate itself from Volvo dealers? (5) Is the lack of alternate transportation a disadvantage, given ride-sharing?

Bengkel resmi vs. bengkel spesialis

In Indonesian cities, authorized dealer workshops compete with independent specialists (for example, a workshop that only services European cars or only Japanese MPVs). The independents often win on direct access to the mechanic and on price; dealers win on genuine parts, warranty and booking apps. Build both service packages and decide where each is stronger.

Case 1.2 · Xpresso Lube

Charlie Green, a mechanic, professional bassist and coffee-plantation owner, saw that both oil-change stations and espresso bars were saturated commodity markets with ugly waiting rooms and, sometimes, dishonest upselling. He converted an old gas station near the University of Texas into Xpresso Lube: a carpeted coffee bar instead of a waiting room, lifts (not pits) so staff can show customers what they find, only two stalls, and rides to work for those who don't want to wait. He chose not to compete on speed. Most customers are women and students who value trust. Thirty-two years after it began, the business has closed.

Questions: (1) Describe its service package. (2) Which service characteristics does it illustrate? (3) Which location elements contribute to its success? (4) What other services could be combined with a coffee bar?

Coffee + something, Indonesian style

Car-wash cafés, barbershops with coffee bars and co-working cafés near campuses all bundle an experience with a routine service. Which of Pine and Gilmore's four realms does each add, and does the bundle survive when the founder leaves?

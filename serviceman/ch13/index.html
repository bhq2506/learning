Service Management · Chapter 13

Planning with averages guarantees a queue.

Capacity planning trades the cost of service against the cost of customer waiting. Queuing models predict the waiting for each level of capacity from just two numbers, the arrival rate λ and the service rate μ. This module covers the A/B/C notation, Little's Law, the M/M/1, M/M/c, M/G/1, M/G/∞ and finite-queue models, pooling, and four criteria for choosing capacity.

Xerox and the Model 9200

When a print shop's Xerox 9200 broke down, the shop lost income. Xerox used queuing analysis to balance customers' waiting cost against its own service cost and replaced solo technical representatives with mini teams: the optimum was three representatives per team.

Capacity at its limit

In July 2021, during the Delta wave, hospital bed occupancy across Java rose above 90%, and patients waited in tents, corridors and cars. When λ approaches capacity, waiting does not rise gently; it explodes. This chapter shows why.

Unit 01

Capacity planning and its strategic role

LO1

Capacity is the ability to deliver service over a time period, set by facilities, equipment and labor. Capacity planning determines the types and amounts of these resources needed to carry out the strategic business plan. Horizons range from decades (build a resort hotel) to hours (staff the lunch rush).

Figure 13.1 · The economic trade-off

More capacity raises the cost of service and lowers the cost of waiting. If both can be priced, the best capacity minimizes their sum. Ambulances are seldom busy more than 30% of the time, because waiting costs lives; post offices accept long lines, because waiting is not critical and customers have few alternatives.

Why services find it hard

Services are open systems: arrivals and service times fluctuate, so idle capacity is a reality. Capacity is measured by inputs (rooms, seats) rather than outputs. And customers take part, so congestion changes quality: a full plane annoys passengers, but a crowded nightclub attracts them.

Preemptive capacity

A 500-room luxury hotel feasible at 60% occupancy can pre-empt rivals in a market that supports only one; we seldom see both a Ritz-Carlton and a Four Seasons in a midsized city.

Short-term capacity

Understaffing the lunch hour sends customers who see the queue to competitors: they balk or renege.

Build ahead of demand

Capacity comes in discrete, irreversible chunks (another aircraft, a hotel), so firms often build ahead. AT&T learned with the iPhone that a successful launch without network capacity creates hostile customers.

Example 13.1 · Cookies and Cream, the naive approach

Peak demand: 50 customers an hour, each ordering a sundae, six baked-to-order cookies and a soft drink, and staying 20 minutes at a table. A sheet holds 12 cookies and bakes in 10 minutes; a server needs 6 minutes per order.

  • Chairs (Little's Law, L = λW): 50 × 20/60 = 16.7
  • Cookie sheets: (50 × 6) ÷ (12 × 6 cycles an hour) = 4.17
  • Servers: (50 × 6 min) ÷ 60 min = 5.0

These are only the values of ρ = λ/μ. Planning at the average leaves no slack for variability, so it is infeasible: queues would grow without limit. Example 13.7 (Unit 06) redoes this with a queuing model.

Discrete capacity in Indonesia

A commuter-rail operator adds whole trainsets, an airport adds a terminal, a hospital adds a ward: capacity arrives in lumps, years after the decision. In between, the system runs either crowded or half empty. The naive calculation (passengers ÷ seats per train) tells you the minimum; the queuing models in this chapter tell you how much more you need for acceptable waiting.

Transient vs. steady state

At opening time (or on a sale day) the queue depends on starting conditions: a transient state. Given time, the system settles into statistical equilibrium, a steady state. All the Appendix D equations assume steady state, which real services, with λ changing hourly, seldom reach. The models still serve long-range planning; when their assumptions fail, use simulation (chapter supplement).

Unit 03

Single-server models: M/M/1 and the finite queue

LO3

The standard M/M/1 assumes: (1) an infinite, independent calling population (no appointments); (2) Poisson arrivals; (3) one line with no length limit, no balking or reneging; (4) FCFS; (5) one server with exponential service times. Every mean has the denominator (μ − λ), so μ must exceed λ: capacity must exceed demand.

Internally consistent

15 min in the system = 9 min waiting + 6 min launching. 1.5 boats in the system = 0.9 in queue + 0.6 being launched (not 1.0: the ramp is busy only 60% of the time).

Sizing a waiting area

How many trailer spaces make sure an arrival finds room 90% of the time? Accumulate Pn until it passes 0.90: n ≤ 4 occurs 92% of the time, so provide room for four (one launching, three waiting).

Finite queue M/M/1 (model V)

At most N in the system; an arrival who finds N leaves. ρ may now exceed 1. P0 = (1 − ρ)/(1 − ρN+1), Pn = P0ρn. Boat ramp with room for two trailers (N = 3): P3 = 0.10, so 6 × 0.10 = 0.6 boaters an hour go elsewhere, and Ls falls to 0.9.

One ATM on campus

An ATM in a faculty lobby is close to M/M/1 at lunchtime. With an illustrative 40 users an hour at about 1.2 minutes each (μ = 50), it is busy 80% of the time and the average wait is about 4.8 minutes. Push λ to 45 and the wait almost doubles, to about 10.8 minutes. Try it in the calculator: the denominator (μ − λ) is why "just a few more users" hurts so much.

Unit 04

Multiple servers, superservers and pooling

LO3LO4

The standard M/M/c has c identical servers fed by one queue; the head of the line goes to the first free server. Steady state needs ρ = λ/μ < c, i.e., the utilization factor λ/cμ < 1. Because the equations are tedious, the book reads Lq from Appendix C; the labs here compute it exactly.

Table 13.2 · Doubling capacity two ways

From a single server at utilization 0.8 (Ls = 4.0, Lq = 3.2): add a second server → Ls = 0.95, Lq = 0.15; or make one superserver twice as fast → Ls = 0.67, Lq = 0.27. The superserver wins on time in the system; parallel servers win on time in the queue. Since customers feel the wait before service most (Chapter 12), reducing Wq is usually advisable, though a 1970s university chose one big mainframe for turnaround time.

The book calls the cut from Ls = 4 to about 1 a "400 percent reduction"; strictly it is a 76% reduction (a fourfold drop).

Example 13.4 · The secretarial pool

Four departmental secretaries, μ = 4 jobs an hour each. Arrivals: 3 an hour for accounting, 2 for the others. Separate M/M/1 queues: accounting waits Ws = 1/(4 − 3) = 60 min, the others 1/(4 − 2) = 30 min. Pool them (M/M/4, λ = 9, ρ = 2.25): Lq = 0.31, Ls = 2.56, Ws = 2.56/9 = 17 min for everyone, with no extra staff.

Try it · Separate queues vs. one pooled queue

Arrival rate per server's own queue (per hour)

Why pooling works: congestion comes from variation. With separate queues, one server can have a line while another sits idle. A single queue never lets a server go idle while someone waits. The same idea explains single lines in banks and post offices (McDonald's dropped it, fearing a long single line would make customers balk).

Caution: if customers must travel to the pooled facility, add travel time. Emergency services are usually dispersed to cut response time.

Pool the calls, disperse the ambulances

Surabaya's Command Center 112 pools emergency calls from the whole city into one center with many call-takers: a pooled M/M/c. The ambulances and fire units it dispatches stay spread across the city, because travel time dominates their response. A Kelurahan or Samsat office that replaces one line per counter with a numbered single queue is pooling too.

Unit 05

Service variability (M/G/1) and self-service (M/G/∞)

LO3LO4
M/G/1 (model III)

Lq = [ρ² + λ²V(t)] / [2(1 − ρ)],  ρ = λE(t) < 1

Any service-time distribution with mean E(t) and variance V(t). The waiting line grows directly with V(t). Put V(t) = 1/μ² (exponential) and you get M/M/1's Lq = ρ²/(1 − ρ). Put V(t) = 0 (constant, M/D/1) and Lq = ρ²/[2(1 − ρ)]: exactly half.

So half the congestion in an M/M/1 comes from service-time variation and half from arrival variation. Limited fast-food menus cut the first; appointments and reservations cut the second.

M/G/∞ self-service (model IV)

Pn = e−ρρn/n!,  Ls = ρ

With unlimited servers (or customers who serve themselves), no one waits; the number in the system is Poisson with mean ρ, for any service distribution. It also approximates systems where waiting is rare, such as sizing an ambulance fleet so that the chance a call finds none free is very small.

Example 13.5, supermarket: 30 shoppers an hour shop for 20 min (M/G/∞: Ls = 10), then queue for 3 registers at 5 min each (M/M/3, ρ = 2.5: Lq = 3.5, Ls = 6). Total: 16 shoppers in the store, 32 minutes each.

Cutting service variability

Cashless toll cards, a standard document checklist posted at a Samsat counter, a warung that sells only three dishes at lunch: each makes service time more predictable, which the M/G/1 formula rewards directly with a shorter queue at the same average speed.

Unit 06

Capacity planning criteria

LO5

In the long run, capacity must exceed demand. If it doesn't, the system adjusts in uncontrolled ways: customers renege, potential customers stay away, and servers speed up, first becoming curt and then cutting service features. Rational planning picks capacity with one of four criteria.

1 · Average waiting time

Keep the mean wait under a target (e.g., 5 minutes, the clock-face increment people notice). Ex. 13.6 drive-in bank: 30 an hour, 3-min transactions, separate lanes; with 3 tellers Wb = 1/(20 − 10) = 6 min; 4 tellers → 4.8 min.

2 · Probability of excessive waiting

P% of customers wait less than T. Ambulances: 95% of urban calls under 10 minutes. Ex. 13.8 gas station: 24 cars an hour, 5 min each; a free pump 95% of the time needs 6 pumps (P(n ≥ 6) = 0.02).

3 · Minimum total cost

TC = Csc + CwLs (or Lq), for captive customers whose wait is a wage. Ex. 13.9 workstations: 8 requests an hour, 15 min each, $10 rent, $30 engineer: 4 workstations, $45.1 an hour.

4 · Sales lost to a small waiting area

A finite queue loses λPN customers per hour. Ex. 13.10 parking lot: 6 spaces, 10 cars an hour, 1-hour stays: P6 = 0.48, so 4.8 cars an hour find it full.

Table 13.5 prints P(n ≥ 4) = 0.27 for four pumps; the M/M/c formula gives 0.17. The decision (six pumps) is unaffected. Linear waiting costs are a simplification: long waits anger customers out of proportion and can lose them for good.

Which criterion would you use?

An SPBU on a busy road: probability of waiting (cars that queue onto the road are a hazard and drive on). A hospital pharmacy for outpatients: average wait, with a published service standard. A firm's internal IT help desk: total cost, since the waiting employees are on the payroll. A mall car park: lost sales, because drivers who see the FULL sign shop elsewhere.

Service benchmark · Don't guesstimate, simulate!

AT&T uses simulation beyond queuing models: a business simulation for its Strategy Execution Initiative, with employee teams competing on cross-selling and web capabilities, and quarterly hands-on disaster-recovery drills in the US, UK and Germany that rebuild damaged network infrastructure in trailers.

Unit 07

Key terms and cases

Case 13.1 · Houston Port Authority

A dockworker crew unloads wheat hopper cars in 30 minutes on average ($50 an hour), 12 cars arrive per 8-hour shift, and the railroad charges $15 an hour demurrage. Options: add a second crew (two cars at once), or install a $400,000 pneumatic system that handles a constant 3 cars an hour with one $15-an-hour operator. With a 10% discount rate and a 10-year horizon, what do you recommend? Hint: crews are M/M/1 or M/M/2; the pneumatic system is M/D/1.

Case 13.2 · Freedom Express

Planes arrive at Reagan National at 20 an hour; the tower lands 60 an hour in clear weather and 30 in bad weather (Poisson). Fuel costs $2.80 a gallon at 20 gallons a minute. How much extra fuel does bad weather cost? How much reserve covers the stack 19 times in 20? Should FreeEx divert to Dulles (combined 40 an hour for two airports at 30 each, $200 bus per diverted flight)?

Case 13.3 · Renaissance Clinic (A)

30 patients an hour see a receptionist (μ = 40); two-thirds go to a nurse clinician (μ = 30), 15% of whom also see the physician (μ = 15); the rest go straight to the physician. Treat each station as an independent M/M/1 to estimate waits, time in system for each path, and idle time; then assess adding a second doctor and a priority rule based on entry time.

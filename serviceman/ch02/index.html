Service Management · Chapter 2

Strategy is deciding who you serve and how you will win.

Service firms face low entry barriers, easy imitation and few economies of scale. This module shows how to formulate a strategic service vision, choose a generic strategy, analyze an industry, understand how customers choose, use information as a weapon, scale and sustain a service, and judge how competitive an operation really is.

Start studyingTry the yield-management lab

Gig platforms changed the rules

Uber showed that a service firm can scale without owning cars or employing drivers: an app matches customers with independent providers and keeps a commission. The strategic questions remain: who is the target market, what is the service concept, how are quality and cost controlled?

Indonesia's version

Gojek began as an ojek call center (2010), launched its app in 2015, and grew into rides, food, payments and logistics. In 2018 Uber sold its Southeast Asian business to Grab. Strategy, not just technology, decided who survived.

Unit 01

The strategic service vision

LO1

A service firm starts with an entrepreneur's idea and an unmet need. Heskett, Sasser and Schlesinger's strategic service vision (Table 2.1) frames it as questions across four elements. Between each pair, ask whether one supports the next. For a start-up, work right to left: begin with the target market.

Service delivery system

Important features (people, technology)? Capacity? How does it differentiate (quality, price, convenience)? Does it create barriers to entry?

Operating strategy

Key business features (operations, finance, marketing, organization)? Where are effort and investment focused? How are quality and cost controlled? Expected results vs. competition?

Service concept

How are results stated in terms of the customer? How should customers and employees perceive it? What effort is needed in design, marketing and delivery?

Target market segment

Common characteristics? How to segment (demographics)? How important is each segment? How well, and by whom, are its needs served now?

Unit 02

The competitive environment and three generic strategies

LO2

Service industries are fragmented, full of small and medium firms. Seven features make competition hard, and they explain why the generic strategies look the way they do in services.

FeatureWhat it meansIndonesian illustration
Low entry barriersInnovations can't be patented and most services aren't capital-intensive, so they are easily copied. (Exception: the best beach site for a resort.)A successful es kopi susu recipe is copied by dozens of kiosks within months.
Minimal economies of scaleCustomers must travel, so outlets stay small. Franchising and the Internet help.A warung serves one neighborhood; a franchise shares purchasing and advertising.
Erratic sales fluctuationsDemand varies by hour, day and season, with random arrivals.Ramadan and Lebaran swing demand for restaurants, transport and retail.
No size advantage with buyers or suppliersSmall firms bargain weakly (exceptions: McDonald's beef, Marriott mattresses).A single café pays more for milk than a national chain.
Product substitutionProducts can replace services (home pregnancy test).Home blood-pressure monitors and test kits reduce clinic visits.
Customer loyaltyPersonalized service builds a loyal base that blocks entrants (supplier terminals at hospitals).A family's long-time tailor, barber or bengkel.
Exit barriersMarginal firms keep going for family employment or hobby appeal.A family warung that employs relatives survives on thin margins, frustrating profit-driven rivals.

Overall cost leadership

Efficient-scale facilities, tight cost and overhead control, often innovative technology; usually high capital investment, aggressive pricing and start-up losses (McDonald's, Walmart, Southwest).

Seek low-cost customers

USAA serves only military families comfortable with phone, mail and online business: no sales force. Costco targets bulk buyers who self-serve.

ID · Digital-only banks serve customers who do everything on the phone, with no branch network.

Standardize a custom service

H&R Block does routine tax returns; storefront legal services and urgent-care clinics. Key word: routine. Danger: product substitutes (TurboTax).

ID · Fixed-price medical check-up packages at laboratory chains.

Reduce the personal element

High-risk, but accepted when it adds convenience: ATMs replaced tellers.

ID · Mobile banking and cash-deposit machines handle most routine transactions once done at the teller counter.

Reduce network costs

FedEx's hub-and-spoke network: each new city adds only one route to and from Memphis.

ID · Couriers route parcels through regional sorting hubs; airlines concentrate flights at Jakarta and Surabaya.

Take operations offline

Decouple work that doesn't need the customer: shoe-repair kiosks feeding a central factory; offshore call centers. Run the back office like a factory.

ID · Laundry counters in malls and apartments feed one central washing plant.

Differentiation

Create a service perceived as unique: brand image, technology, features, customer service, dealer network. Cost isn't ignored, but the thrust is loyalty, at a price the target customer will pay.

Make the intangible tangible

Branded hotel toiletries; Hartford Steam Boiler adding inspections to insurance.

ID · A workshop sends before-and-after photos of the repair over WhatsApp.

Customize the standard product

Greeting guests by name; salons vs. barbershops; Burger King's made-to-order.

ID · Choosing sugar level and ice in your es teh; a hotel that remembers your pillow preference.

Reduce perceived risk

Explain the work, keep a clean facility, guarantee it (Village Volvo). Peace of mind is worth paying for.

ID · Taxi firms built reputations on metered fares and honest drivers, which mattered most to first-time visitors.

Give attention to training

Hard to replicate. McDonald's Hamburger University.

ID · Large banks and airlines run in-house training centers for frontline staff.

Control quality

Training, procedures, technology, limited scope, supervision, peer pressure. Magic Pan's foolproof crêpe machine.

ID · Drink chains use automatic machines and fixed recipes so every outlet tastes the same.

Focus

Serve a particular target market very well: a buyer group (USAA), a service (Shouldice, hernias), budget travelers (Motel 6), guaranteed overnight delivery (FedEx) or a region (a community college). Focus is differentiation and/or cost leadership applied to one segment.

Davidow and Uttal stress customer selection: (1) segment the market to design core services, (2) classify customers by the value they place on service, (3) set expectations slightly below perceived performance. A Palo Alto bank even closes accounts after two bounced checks.

ID · Focus + cost

BRI built its franchise on micro-lending through thousands of small village units; BTPN Syariah serves women micro-entrepreneurs through community groups.

ID · Focus + differentiation

Specialist eye hospitals do one family of procedures at high volume, the same logic as Shouldice's hernia focus.

Unit 03

Strategic analysis: Five Forces and SWOT

LO3

Start with an objective ("should we enter this industry with a new service?"). Use Porter's Five Forces at the industry level to judge competitive intensity and attractiveness, then SWOT at the firm level.

Competitive rivalry (center)

Often the major determinant. Price or non-price rivalry; industry capacity vs. demand. Netflix's mail-order DVDs faced only rental stores such as Blockbuster.

Potential new entrants

High returns invite entry. Walmart challenged Netflix but left, unable to beat its brand.

Threat of substitutes

For services, often a product: TurboTax vs. an accountant. Netflix later faced streaming (Amazon Prime on smart TVs).

Bargaining power of suppliers

Unique inputs or monopoly sources. Netflix had leverage over DVD distributors through volume.

Bargaining power of customers

Price pressure; Priceline and Hotwire shifted information asymmetry to travelers. Netflix's recommendations stimulate demand.

SWOT

Internal strengths and weaknesses, external opportunities and threats. Conclude with strengths to leverage, weaknesses to remedy, opportunities to capture, threats to counter. Subjective, so do it as a team.

Try it · Rate the five forces

Presets are teaching judgments for discussion, not research findings. 1 = weak force (good for profits), 5 = strong force.

Unit 04

Winning customers: qualifiers, winners and losers

LO4

Customers choose using competitive criteria. Adding a new criterion is itself differentiation: American Airlines' AAdvantage program added loyalty to airline competition.

Availability

How accessible? ATMs and 24/7 websites.

Convenience

Location on busy streets: fuel stations, fast food, dry cleaners.

Dependability

Reliable? Bugs returning after the exterminator; fixing the car on the first visit; on-time flights.

Personalization

Treated as an individual? Greeting repeat guests by name.

Price

Less effective than for goods; in professional services price may signal quality.

Quality

Expectations vs. perceptions, judged on process and outcome.

Reputation

Word of mouth resolves uncertainty; a bad service can't be returned.

Safety

Customers put their lives in the provider's hands: air travel, medicine.

Speed

Response time is the key measure for emergency services.

The purchase decision (after Terry Hill)

1 · Qualifiers

The minimum level to be taken seriously, defined by other players. Airline safety; cleanliness in fast food. Yesterday's winners (drive-through) become today's qualifiers.

2 · Service winners

The criteria used to choose among qualified firms, varying by occasion: convenience for lunch, reputation for a dinner date.

3 · Service losers

Falling below expectations loses the customer forever. Dependability, personalization and speed are especially vulnerable.

Choosing a restaurant in Surabaya

Qualifier: for most Muslim diners, halal assurance (being phased in as a legal requirement for food businesses) and basic cleanliness. Winner: for a quick lunch, walking distance and price; for a family celebration, reputation, ambience and a private room. Loser: a 45-minute wait, a wrong order on a delivery app, or rude staff. The same criterion can play different roles for different customers and occasions.

Where you meet yield management

Airline fares on travel apps that rise as the flight fills, hotel rates that jump on long weekends, and train fares that move within an upper and lower band set by the operator. Like American's SABRE, the goal is that no seat goes empty for want of a bargain hunter, while some are held for late full-fare buyers. Chapter 11 covers the method in detail.

Using information to categorize customers

Coding

Grade customers by profitability; codes tell staff how to handle each.

Routing

Call centers queue by code: big spenders to senior problem-solvers; others may never reach a person.

Targeting

Fees waived and hidden discounts for valuable customers.

Sharing

Transaction history shared with other firms as a revenue source.

Priority banking

Major Indonesian banks run priority-banking tiers for customers above a balance threshold: separate lounges, a dedicated relationship manager, a priority call-center line and fee waivers. That is coding, routing and targeting in one program. Ask: is it fair to the customer in the regular queue?

Limits in the use of information

Invasion of privacy

Cloud storage and data mining can expose lifestyle, medical and financial details.

Data security

Records released without consent can be used to blackball employees or patients.

Reliability

Corrupt data create havoc; people need the right to review records.

Anticompetitive

Reservation systems and frequent-user programs can block competition.

Fairness

Under yield management, two passengers in adjacent seats may pay very different fares.

ID · The law caught up

Indonesia's Personal Data Protection Law (UU 27/2022) requires a lawful basis and consent for processing personal data and gives individuals rights to access and correct it, after several high-profile alleged data leaks.

Unit 06

The virtual value chain, data analytics and IoT

LO6

Firms compete in a physical marketplace and a virtual marketspace of information. In the virtual value chain, information is not just support: it creates value. Every stage involves five activities: gather, organize, select, synthesize, distribute. FedEx's package tracking was the classic first step.

USAA's four-stage evolution

Reading Gojek through the same lens (our interpretation)

New processes: a phone call center replaced by app dispatch with GPS matching. New knowledge: every trip records where people travel, when and how they pay. New products: that knowledge supported food delivery, parcels and the GoPay wallet. New relationships: merchants and drivers became co-creators, using platform tools to manage their own businesses.

Data analytics

The discovery, interpretation and processing of meaningful patterns in data for better decisions, combining statistics, programming and operations research. Big data bring four components to manage: volume, variety, velocity, variability.

Descriptive

What happened?

Last month's average waiting time at each puskesmas.

Diagnostic

Why did it happen?

Waits spike on Monday mornings when the registration system is slow.

Predictive

What will happen?

Forecasting patient arrivals in the week after Lebaran.

Prescriptive

What should we do?

An optimized staff roster that meets a 30-minute waiting-time target.

Internet of Things (IoT)

Devices with sensors (collect data) and actuators (act on it), connected through the Internet: the alarm clock that checks traffic, the rice cooker that times itself. Built on a trajectory from RFID onward; it converges wireless communication, real-time analytics, machine learning and embedded systems.

Internet of Medical Things (IoMT)

Remote monitoring of chronic patients, connected infusion pumps, sensor beds, tracking medication orders. Drawbacks: overloading physicians with data, and constant security updates.

Economics of scalability and sustainability

LO8LO7

Scalability is the ability to improve contribution margin (revenue minus variable costs) as volume grows. Infinite scalability requires a zero variable cost for each extra customer. Three sources: (1) conduct only information or data-transfer services, (2) let customers serve themselves, (3) let customers serve other customers.

Sustainability and the triple bottom line

Service firms matter as much as factories for sustainability. The triple bottom line (TBL) evaluates a firm on social (labor practices, diversity, human rights, community), economic (capital efficiency, growth, cost, risk) and environmental (air, water, land, emissions, waste) criteria. Shell popularized "People, Planet, Profit"; overlaps are socio-economic (jobs, skills, ethics), eco-efficiency (resource efficiency, life cycle) and social-environmental (climate, environmental justice, health and safety). True sustainability satisfies all three.

Drivers: regulation (EPA, WEEE, RoHS, ISO 14000/26000), image, economics (waste reduction), the ripple effect on customers' choices (a mortgage shapes a community), strategic threats (ski resorts without snow, insurers facing storms, NGO campaigns) and emerging opportunities (Starbucks' grower contracts). Walmart set goals of 100% renewable energy, zero waste and sustainable products; critics question the cost and labor conditions.

ID · Regulation

OJK regulation POJK 51/2017 on sustainable finance requires financial institutions and listed companies to publish sustainability reports.

ID · Planet

Bali's Governor Regulation 97/2018 restricts single-use plastic bags, straws and styrofoam, pushing hotels, restaurants and shops to change packaging.

ID · Strategic threat

Coastal flooding in northern Java and coral bleaching threaten logistics hubs and dive tourism: climate risk is a business risk.

Practice · A hospital's TBL: people, planet or profit?

COVID-19's socio-economic effects

The pandemic required trillions in response packages, risked sustainability setbacks and a cycle of environmental degradation and zoonotic outbreaks, and deepened inequalities by age, gender, education and income. The speed of recovery will shape the social and environmental balance.

Unit 08

Stages in service firm competitiveness

LO9

Chase and Hayes describe four stages by the role operations plays. Firms need not start at stage 1 (FedEx arguably began at stage 3) and can slip back through neglect.

1 · Available for service

Operations is a necessary evil at minimum cost. Customers often have no alternative; workers need direct supervision; technology only when survival demands it.

2 · Journeyman

Competition forces parity: copy industry practice (everyone adds a drive-through). Operations is competitive-neutral; firms look alike.

3 · Distinctive competence

Leaders understand what creates value (SAS's Jan Carlzon and on-time departures). Service guarantees, empowerment, cross-training, Six Sigma.

4 · World-class

Expands customer expectations beyond competitors' reach (Disney, Marriott). Technology as an advantage (FedEx COSMOS tracking); employees identify with the mission.

Try it · Assess a service you know (Table 2.7)

Pick the description that best fits each dimension, for example your bank, a hospital, or a public service office. The profile shows where operations helps or holds back competitiveness.

From "available for service" upward

Many public services in Indonesia once fit stage 1: one counter, no alternative, cash only, long queues. One-stop public service malls (Mal Pelayanan Publik), online queue numbers and app-based renewals show agencies moving toward stage 2 or 3. Which stage is your campus's academic administration office? What one change would move it up?

Unit 09

Key terms and cases

Case 2.1 · United Commercial Bank and El Banco de Nuestra Comunidad

Two banks focused on ethnic communities. UCB served San Francisco's Chinese American community: high-interest savings and time deposits, very low fees, conservative real-estate lending (58% average loan-to-value), trade finance for importers, Chinese-speaking staff and cultural touches such as tea sets. El Banco, a licensed franchise inside existing banks, served Atlanta's fast-growing Latino community with a lively, Spanish-language setting and fee-based services: check cashing for people with or without accounts, ITIN-based mortgages and a Western Union-style approach to winning trust. The ending: in 2009 UCB failed and was merged into East West Bank; two executives were later charged with hiding loan losses.

Questions: compare the two strategic service visions; identify the winners, qualifiers and losers; what differentiates banks serving ethnic communities; could a niche clientele support a bank exclusively?

Indonesian parallels

Islamic banks serve customers who want sharia-compliant products; rural banks (BPR) and cooperatives serve village communities; micro-finance banks serve women's groups. Pick one and build its strategic service vision. What would be its qualifier, its winner and its most dangerous loser? What does UCB's ending teach about focus without governance?

Case 2.2 · The Alamo Drafthouse

A cinema in Austin, Texas, that serves food and drink during films. The owners' first theater in Bakersfield failed; in Austin (1996) they designed "intentionally minimal" service: customers write orders on paper and staff avoid interrupting the movie. By 2021 there were five Drafthouses in Austin.

Questions: position it on a market map; use the strategic service vision; identify qualifiers, winners and losers; apply the Five Forces; conduct a SWOT.
